How to Shoot Vertical & Horizontal on the Same Trip: A Complete Field Guide

If you have ever come home from a two-week road trip with 4,000 horizontal photos and nothing to post on Instagram Reels, you already know the pain. Conversely, if you shot everything vertically for TikTok and now want a wide cinematic YouTube video or a print for your wall, you are stuck with a column of skinny clips that look terrible on a 16:9 timeline.

The solution is not to choose one orientation. It is to deliberately capture both — vertical and horizontal — from the same trip, often from the same spot, without doubling your shooting time or annoying your travel companions. I have done this across the American Southwest, the Pacific Northwest, and several national parks, and I have made almost every mistake in this article so you do not have to.

This tutorial walks you through the entire workflow: gear, planning, on-location technique, and post-trip organization. By the end, you will have a repeatable system for capturing both formats on a single trip.

Prerequisites: What You Need Before You Start

You do not need a cinema camera to do this. A modern smartphone, a mirrorless camera, or even an action camera can work. But a few things make the process dramatically easier.

  • A camera that shoots both orientations without menu diving. Any recent Sony (a7 IV, a7C II), Canon (R6 Mark II, R8), Nikon (Z6 III), Fujifilm (X-T5, X-S20), or a flagship phone (iPhone 15/16 Pro, Samsung Galaxy S24 Ultra, Google Pixel 9 Pro) will do. If your camera has a flip-out screen, you are already ahead.
  • A quick-release plate or cage that lets you rotate. A standard Arca-Swiss plate works. A dedicated L-bracket is even better because it lets you switch from landscape to portrait on a tripod without re-leveling.
  • A gimbal or stabilizer that supports both orientations. The DJI RS 3 Mini, DJI RS 4, or Zhiyun Weebill 3S all support vertical mounting. If you use a phone, the DJI Osmo Mobile 6 or Insta360 Flow Pro rotate natively.
  • Extra storage. Shooting both orientations roughly doubles your data. Bring at least two 128GB V30 or faster SD cards (SanDisk Extreme Pro or Lexar Professional 1667x are reliable and widely available at Best Buy and B&H).
  • A way to label clips on the fly. A small notebook, a voice memo habit, or a camera that writes orientation metadata (most modern cameras do).
  • A basic editing setup. Adobe Premiere Pro, DaVinci Resolve (free), Final Cut Pro, or CapCut. You will need to manage mixed aspect ratios in the timeline.

One more prerequisite: decide why you need both. If you are only posting to Instagram, you may only need vertical. If you are delivering to a client who wants a YouTube video and a Reel, you need both. Knowing your deliverables before you leave home prevents wasted effort.

Step-by-Step Guide: Shooting Both Orientations on One Trip

  1. Step 1: Define Your Deliverables Before You Pack

    Write down exactly what you need to produce. For example: "One 8-minute YouTube video (horizontal), three Instagram Reels (vertical), and a photo set for a blog post (horizontal + a few vertical for Pinterest)." This list determines how much of each orientation you shoot. If you skip this step, you will either overshoot one format or undershoot the other. On a trip to Zion National Park, I once shot 90% horizontal because I assumed I would only make a YouTube video. Two weeks later, a brand asked for vertical clips. I had almost nothing usable. Do not repeat my mistake.

  2. Step 2: Choose the Right Gear Configuration

    Your gear should let you switch orientation in under 10 seconds. Here is what I recommend for U.S. travelers:

    • Mirrorless camera route: Sony a7C II or Canon R8 with a compact zoom like the Sony 20-70mm f/4 or Canon RF 24-105mm f/4L. Add a SmallRig L-bracket. This combo fits in a Peak Design Everyday Sling 6L.
    • Phone-first route: iPhone 16 Pro or Pixel 9 Pro with a Moment or Peak Design mobile tripod and a DJI Osmo Mobile 6. This is the lightest option and works well for solo travel.
    • Hybrid route (my usual): Mirrorless for horizontal hero shots, phone for vertical social clips. This splits the workload and keeps you fast.
    • Action camera route: GoPro Hero 13 Black or DJI Osmo Action 5 Pro. Both shoot vertical natively when you rotate the camera, and the footage is already stabilized.

    Whatever you choose, test the rotation before you leave. A gimbal that takes 30 seconds to rebalance will kill your motivation to switch on location.

  3. Step 3: Plan Each Location for Both Formats

    Not every scene works in both orientations. A sweeping Grand Canyon vista is naturally horizontal. A narrow slot canyon in Antelope Canyon, Arizona, is naturally vertical. Plan your shot list accordingly.

    For each location, ask:

    • What is the hero subject? (A person, a landmark, a detail)
    • Does the environment have strong vertical lines (trees, skyscrapers, waterfalls) or horizontal lines (coastlines, prairies, skylines)?
    • Can I shoot the same subject twice in 60 seconds?

    In practice, I use a simple rule: shoot the wide establishing shot horizontally, then immediately shoot a tight vertical version of the same subject. This gives me a matched pair for editing.

  4. Step 4: Master the 30-Second Orientation Switch

    Speed is everything. Here is the exact sequence I use with a mirrorless camera on a tripod:

    1. Shoot the horizontal wide shot. Lock exposure and focus.
    2. Loosen the L-bracket or ball head, rotate the camera 90 degrees to portrait, and re-tighten.
    3. Do not re-level the tripod if you are using an L-bracket — the rotation is centered.
    4. Reframe. Because your field of view changes dramatically, you will need to move the camera or change focal length.
    5. Shoot the vertical version. Consider shooting a slightly wider focal length so you have room to crop in post.

    With a phone or gimbal, the switch is even faster — often just a physical rotation. Practice this at home until it is muscle memory.

  5. Step 5: Use the "Two-Pass" Shooting Method

    Instead of switching back and forth constantly, shoot in two passes per location:

    • Pass 1 (horizontal): Capture all your wide, cinematic, and establishing shots. This is your "A-roll" for YouTube or client work.
    • Pass 2 (vertical): Walk the same path again and capture tight, vertical, social-first clips. Focus on faces, hands, details, and movement.

    This method is faster than alternating because you stay in one mental mode. It also produces cleaner footage because you are not constantly re-balancing gear. I used this approach on a shoot in Seattle's Pike Place Market and captured a full YouTube segment plus six Reels in under 90 minutes.

  6. Step 6: Frame for Safe Cropping

    If you want to shoot once and crop later, you can — but you must frame for it. Place your subject in the center third of the frame and leave generous headroom and side room. Then, in post, you can crop a 16:9 clip to 9:16 without cutting off heads.

    This works best with:

    • Static or slow-moving shots
    • Subjects with space around them
    • Scenes without critical edge details

    It works poorly with fast action, wide landscapes, and any shot where the edges matter. Use cropping as a backup, not your primary strategy.

  7. Step 7: Capture Vertical Audio Separately When Needed

    One overlooked issue: if you shoot horizontal video with a shotgun mic and then shoot vertical video on a phone, your audio quality will be inconsistent. For interviews or narration, record audio separately with a lav mic (Rode Wireless GO II or DJI Mic 2) and sync in post. This keeps your vertical and horizontal clips sounding identical.

  8. Step 8: Label and Organize on the Go

    At the end of each day, spend 10 minutes organizing. Create two folders on your laptop or SSD:

    • YYYY-MM-DD_Location_Horizontal
    • YYYY-MM-DD_Location_Vertical

    If you cannot offload daily, use your camera's rating system or a voice memo to tag orientation. This saves hours in post. On a 10-day trip through Utah and Arizona, daily organization cut my edit time by roughly 40%.

  9. Step 9: Edit Mixed Orientations Without Losing Your Mind

    In your editing software:

    • Create a horizontal sequence (1920x1080 or 3840x2160) for your main video.
    • Create a separate vertical sequence (1080x1920) for social clips.
    • Use adjustment layers or nested sequences to apply color grading consistently across both.
    • If you must use vertical clips in a horizontal video, place them on a blurred background or use a split-screen layout.

    DaVinci Resolve handles mixed aspect ratios well with its "Scale to Fit" and "Crop" options. Premiere Pro users should set up separate sequences from the start.

  10. Step 10: Export for Each Platform

    Export settings that work in the U.S. market:

    • YouTube (horizontal): 3840x2160 or 1920x1080, H.264, 20–50 Mbps, AAC audio.
    • Instagram Reels / TikTok (vertical): 1080x1920, H.264, 10–20 Mbps, 30 or 60 fps.
    • Client delivery: ProRes 422 or H.265 for archival, plus platform-specific exports.

    Always export a clean master in each orientation before you start cutting platform-specific versions.

Troubleshooting Tips from the Field

Problem: My gimbal will not balance in vertical mode.

Check whether your gimbal supports native vertical mounting. The DJI RS 3 Mini and RS 4 do. Older gimbals often require a third-party vertical plate. If yours does not support it, shoot vertical handheld with stabilization in post, or use a phone gimbal for vertical and your main camera for horizontal.

Problem: My vertical clips look soft or noisy.

This usually happens when you crop a 4K horizontal clip to 1080x1920. You are effectively using only about 25% of your sensor. Shoot vertical natively whenever possible, or shoot horizontal in 6K/8K if your camera supports it so you have resolution to spare.

Problem: I keep forgetting to shoot vertical.

Set a recurring phone reminder every two hours during your trip: "Shoot vertical." It sounds silly, but it works. I also set my camera's custom button to switch to a vertical-friendly crop mode, which serves as a visual cue.

Problem: My travel companion is annoyed by the constant switching.

Use the two-pass method and communicate. Tell them: "Give me five minutes here, then we are done." Most people are fine with a short, focused shoot. What annoys them is indecisive fumbling. Practice your switch at home so you look competent on location.

Problem: My footage looks different in color between phone and camera.

Shoot in a flat or log profile on both if possible, or use a matching LUT in post. If you are mixing iPhone footage with a Sony or Canon camera, expect to spend time color matching. Shooting everything on one device is the simplest fix.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Shooting only one orientation "just in case." You will regret it. If you have deliverables that need both, shoot both.
  • Cropping horizontal footage to vertical for everything. It works for some shots, but it destroys resolution and composition. Use it sparingly.
  • Ignoring audio consistency. Mixed audio quality between vertical and horizontal clips is jarring. Record audio separately for anything important.
  • Not labeling clips. You will spend hours hunting for the right orientation in post. Organize daily.
  • Over-shooting. You do not need 20 vertical clips of the same sunset. Three good ones are enough.
  • Forgetting to check your camera's orientation metadata. Some cameras rotate footage automatically; others do not. Know your camera before you rely on it.
  • Neglecting backup storage. Shooting both orientations doubles your data. Bring more cards and drives than you think you need.

Final Thoughts

Shooting vertical and horizontal on the same trip is not about doing twice the work. It is about planning once, shooting smart, and organizing as you go. With the two-pass method, a fast orientation switch, and a clear deliverable list, you can capture both formats without slowing down your trip or driving your travel companions crazy.

Start with one location on your next trip. Shoot a horizontal wide, then a vertical tight. Edit them side by side. Once you see how much more flexibility you have, you will never go back to single-orientation shooting again.

Now go pack your bag, charge your batteries, and shoot both. Your future self — and your client — will thank you.
