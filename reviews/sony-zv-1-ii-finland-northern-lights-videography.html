Dawid Millennium — Travel Videographer & Founder

Published: November 5, 2026 | Field-tested in Rovaniemi, Finnish Lapland

Filming the Aurora Borealis is the ultimate stress test for any camera sensor. You are dealing with near-pitch darkness, rapidly moving subjects, and freezing temperatures that drain batteries in minutes. While full-frame mirrorless cameras are the traditional choice, I wanted to see if the ultra-portable Sony ZV-1 II could hold its own as a dedicated travel aurora camera.

⚡ Key Takeaways

  • Surprisingly Capable Sensor: The 1-inch sensor captures the green and purple hues of the aurora much better than smartphone cameras or smaller-sensor action cams.
  • Cold Weather Prep is Mandatory: The NP-BX1 battery will die in under 30 minutes at -15°C without proper insulation.
  • Manual Focus is Non-Negotiable: Autofocus will hunt and fail in the dark. You must pre-focus to infinity.

The Low-Light Reality: What the ZV-1 II Can Actually Do

Let’s be clear: the ZV-1 II is not an astrophotography powerhouse. However, its f/1.8 aperture at the wide end and 1-inch sensor gather significantly more light than the tiny sensors in action cameras. When the aurora was highly active (KP index 4+), the camera captured the sweeping green curtains with minimal color noise at ISO 3200. Pushing to ISO 6400 or 12800 introduced visible grain, but the footage remained usable for social media and travel vlogs, especially after basic noise reduction in post.

Battling the Arctic Cold: Battery & Gear Survival

The Battery Drain Problem

Lithium-ion chemistry slows down dramatically in the cold. During my first night out at -10°C, the camera displayed a "low battery" warning after just 25 minutes, despite being fully charged. The Fix: I started keeping spare NP-BX1 batteries inside my inner jacket pocket, using my body heat to keep them warm. I also wrapped the camera body in a thin neoprene sleeve, leaving only the lens and screen exposed. This simple trick doubled the operational time.

Crucial Settings for Northern Lights Videography

If you are bringing the ZV-1 II to Finland, do not rely on Auto mode. Use these manual settings:

  • Mode: Manual (M)
  • Aperture: f/1.8 (widest possible)
  • Shutter Speed: 1/15s to 1/30s (any slower, and the moving aurora will blur into a shapeless glow)
  • ISO: 3200 (push to 6400 only if the aurora is faint)
  • Focus: Manual Focus (MF). Focus on a distant light or the moon during the day, tape the focus ring, and do not touch it at night.
  • SteadyShot: Off (The camera must be on a sturdy tripod; digital stabilization will ruin low-light exposure).

How It Compares to Rugged Alternatives

While the ZV-1 II can capture the aurora, it is not weather-sealed. If you are caught in a snow squall, you are taking a risk. For extreme, wet, and rugged winter conditions where you need to mount the camera to a ski or helmet, the GoPro Hero 13 Black is a safer, more durable bet, though its smaller sensor will struggle more with pure low-light detail.

Final Verdict

The Sony ZV-1 II is a highly capable, ultra-compact option for northern lights videography if you are willing to use a tripod, shoot in manual mode, and manage your battery temperature carefully. It won’t replace a dedicated full-frame astro camera, but for travelers who want to pack light and still capture the magic of Finnish Lapland, it punches well above its weight class.
