Taking the Insta360 X5 Around the World: Heat, Bans & 999-Hour Uploads

We sat down with Dawid Millennium after his 12-country, 3-continent filming trip to talk about earthquakes, giraffes, desert heat, police confiscations — and the cloud upload that said 999 hours.

DM
Interviewee
Dawid Millennium
ET
Interviewer
HVC Editorial Team
Interview by HVC Editorial Team | Published August 29, 2026 | 15 min read
Interviews
Editor's Note

When Dawid Millennium returned from his round-the-world filming trip, his memory cards contained giraffes in Nairobi, thermal shutdown warnings from Rio, and a cloud upload estimate of 999 hours. His companion for the entire journey: an Insta360 X5 bought in Bangkok two days after an earthquake. We sat down with him to get the full, unfiltered story of what happens when one 360 camera meets the real world.

Part 1: The Earthquake & The Decision

Q

Let's start at the beginning. You bought the Insta360 X5 in Bangkok — right after an earthquake. What happened?

DM Dawid Millennium

Yeah, it sounds like the opening of a movie, but it's true. The earthquake shook Bangkok, and two days later I walked into a camera shop and bought the X5. Something about feeling the ground move makes you realize how fast everything can change. I thought: I want to record everything. Not just one trip — the whole world. Every angle, no misses. That's the beauty of a 360 camera. You never miss the moment behind you.

So I made the decision right there: this isn't a holiday anymore, it's a global video project. Twelve countries, three continents, one camera.

Q

You shot the entire trip in PURE VIDEO mode. For readers who don't know — what exactly is that?

DM Dawid Millennium

PURE VIDEO is the X5's super high-quality lighting and shooting option. Think of it as the camera's cinema mode — maximum dynamic range, minimum noise, the cleanest possible 360 footage. In good light it's genuinely stunning.

But here's the trade-off, and this became the theme of my whole trip: bigger files, faster battery drain, and more heat. A lot more heat. Almost every problem I had on this journey traces back to that trade-off.

Part 2: Africa & The Internet Problem

Q

First stop on our tour: Nairobi, Kenya. You filmed at the Giraffe Center. How did the camera perform?

DM Dawid Millennium

Camera-wise? Perfect. Around 24°C, mild and comfortable. The Giraffe Center sits next to Nairobi National Park — a UNESCO-recognized wildlife area right on the edge of the capital — and the 360 format was born for that place. Giraffes approaching from every direction, and I never had to turn the camera. Every spot, every eyelash, captured in PURE VIDEO.

The problem was the internet. I tried uploading to Insta360 Cloud after the session and the progress bar barely moved. I waited 45 minutes and gave up. In East Africa, the cloud simply doesn't exist for 360 files. You need local storage — a portable SSD, full stop.

In Nairobi the camera was perfect. The internet was impossible. In Dubai it was the exact opposite.

Part 3: South America & The 999-Hour Upload

Q

Then Brazil. We've heard rumors of a "999 hours" upload. Please tell us this is not real.

DM Dawid Millennium

(laughs) It's real. Rio's internet was actually better than Nairobi's — I could push some footage to the cloud. But the heat was brutal, 38 degrees and climbing. About 30 minutes into a session near Copacabana, the camera got hot to the touch, flashed a temperature warning, and switched itself off. Automatically. No negotiation.

Then, when I tried uploading the remaining files to Insta360 Cloud, the app showed me an estimated time of 999 hours. Nine hundred and ninety-nine. I stared at it for a full minute. It's not a bug — it's the app's way of saying "this file is too big for this connection." Lesson learned: upload at night, on WiFi, and split your files.

Q

Did things improve in Peru, Colombia, and Costa Rica?

DM Dawid Millennium

Much better. Peru and Colombia had solid internet — 20 to 40 minutes per 360 file on hotel WiFi. Not instant, but workable. The camera was happy too; temperatures in the highlands were manageable, so no overheating.

But the surprise winner of the entire trip was San José, Costa Rica. I pushed a full 360 video to the cloud in under 10 minutes. The capital's fiber infrastructure is seriously impressive. If you're a traveling creator who depends on cloud backup, San José is your best friend.

Part 4: The Heat Kills Cameras — Dubai & Maldives

Q

Dubai. 40 degrees. What happened?

DM Dawid Millennium

Forget using the X5 outdoors in Dubai during the day. The desert hit 40 Celsius and the camera switched itself off within 15 minutes. PURE VIDEO generates extra heat, so I was basically carrying a small heater in my hand.

Here's the irony: Dubai had the fastest internet of the entire trip. Uploads that took 40 minutes in Colombia finished in under 5 minutes. Record-fast. But what's the point of the world's fastest upload when the camera can't survive long enough to record the footage?

Q

And the Maldives — paradise, right?

DM Dawid Millennium

Paradise for your eyes, hell for your camera. Equatorial sun, high humidity, white reflective sand — the X5 overheated in about 10 minutes. I had to film in 8-minute bursts, then let it cool in the shade for 15 minutes, then go again. Doable, but you can't just hit record and wander around for an hour like you can in Bali.

Part 5: The Good Zones & The Forbidden Zones

Q

Was there anywhere you could simply record for hours?

DM Dawid Millennium

Two places: Bali and Hanoi. Cloud cover, shade, moderate temperatures — I recorded 30 to 45 minute sessions without a single shutdown. If your project needs long-form 360 recording, those cities are the answer. They're the good zones.

Q

Now the serious part. You mentioned legal trouble at the Macao–Zhuhai border and in Da Nang.

DM Dawid Millennium

This is the part people need to hear. At the Chinese border crossing between Macao and Zhuhai, 360 cameras are effectively forbidden. The police and border authorities will not let you use one — even for a moment. The penalty is a heavy fine taken straight from your bank account, or confiscation of the camera. Like a drone, treated like a drone.

And in Da Nang, Vietnam, drones are banned outright — I watched them get confiscated on the beach. A 360 camera on a selfie stick attracts the same suspicion. My rule now: research local filming laws before you fly, not after you land. In some countries a 360 camera needs a permit. In some, it's simply illegal.

Part 6: The Verdict

Q

Final question. After 12 countries and 3 continents — what's your score for the Insta360 X5?

DM Dawid Millennium

7.2 out of 10. Image quality in PURE VIDEO: 9 out of 10 when conditions are right. Heat management: 5. Cloud ecosystem in slow-internet regions: 4. It's a brilliant camera when the world cooperates — and the world often doesn't cooperate.

My advice: know your destination before you pack it. Film in the right conditions and it's magic. Push it into 40-degree deserts, or past border police who treat it like a drone, and it becomes an expensive paperweight. The camera never lied to me. The spec sheet just left out the parts about heat, internet, and laws.

⚡ Quick Fire Round

Best location for the X5?
Bali. No contest.
Worst location?
Dubai at noon. The camera lasted 15 minutes. I lasted longer.
One accessory you'd add to the kit?
A portable SSD. The cloud is a myth in half the world.
Would you do the trip again?
Tomorrow. But I'd film at sunrise, upload at night, and check every embassy website first.
