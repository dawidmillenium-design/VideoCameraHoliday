How to Film in Harsh Sunlight: Travel Video Tips & Camera Settings

Stop fighting the sun. Learn ND filters, exposure lock, and positioning to get cinematic travel footage even at midday.

In Short

  • Midday sun creates blown-out skies and harsh shadows. Use ND filters to maintain proper shutter speed.
  • Position your subject with the sun behind them or use open shade to soften facial lighting.
  • Lock your exposure on the brightest part of the frame to prevent flickering when subjects move.
  • Use a lens hood or your hand to flag direct light hitting the front element.
  • Recover details in post using highlight recovery tools, but prevention is always better than fixing.

You’ve planned the perfect beach day, but the moment you hit record at noon, your footage looks flat, washed out, and unwatchable. Harsh sunlight is the number one enemy of travel videography. But with the right techniques and gear, you can turn brutal midday light into cinematic gold.

The Golden Rule: Don't Fight the Sun

The biggest mistake travelers make is trying to expose for everything at once. In harsh sunlight, your camera’s dynamic range will be exceeded. Instead of fighting it, work with the light. If the sun is directly overhead, embrace high-contrast silhouettes or seek open shade. Never point your lens directly at the midday sun unless you’re intentionally creating a flare effect.

Problem 1: Overexposed Skies

When your sky is pure white with no detail, your video loses depth and realism. This happens because your camera is exposing for the darker foreground.

Solution: Use an ND (Neutral Density) filter. An ND filter acts like sunglasses for your lens, allowing you to keep your shutter speed at 1/50 (for 24fps) or 1/60 (for 30fps) without overexposing. Start with an ND8 or ND16 for bright beach days.

Problem 2: Harsh Shadows

Direct overhead sun creates raccoon-eye shadows under brows and noses, making portraits look unflattering.

Solution: Move your subject into "open shade" — under a palm tree, umbrella, or building overhang. The light is still bright but diffused. If you must shoot in direct sun, position the sun behind your subject and use a reflector (or a white t-shirt) to bounce light back onto their face.

Problem 3: Lens Flare

Unwanted ghosting and haze occur when stray light hits the front lens element at an angle.

Solution: Always use a lens hood. For action cameras like the GoPro Hero 13, use the official lens hood accessory. As a quick fix, cup your hand above the lens to create a makeshift flag.

Camera Settings for Sunlight

  • Shutter Speed: Always double your frame rate. 1/50 for 24fps, 1/60 for 30fps. Use ND filters to achieve this in bright light.
  • ISO: Keep it as low as possible (ISO 100 or base ISO). Bright sun means you rarely need to raise it.
  • Aperture: If using a mirrorless like the Sony ZV-1 II, stop down to f/5.6 or f/8 to increase depth of field and reduce overexposure.
  • Exposure Lock: Tap and hold on the brightest part of your scene (usually the sky or sand) to lock AE. This prevents the camera from constantly readjusting as people walk through the frame.

Best Cameras for Bright Conditions

Not all sensors handle high dynamic range equally. Here are our top picks for harsh sunlight:

  • GoPro Hero 13: Excellent HDR mode and built-in ND filter support. Handles extreme contrast beautifully.
  • DJI Osmo Pocket 3: 1-inch sensor captures more shadow detail. Pair with an external ND filter for best results.
  • iPhone 16 Pro: Smart HDR 5 automatically balances highlights and shadows. Enable "Photographic Styles" for consistent color in bright light.

Practical Walkthrough: Filming at the Beach at Noon

  1. Attach an ND16 filter to your camera.
  2. Set shutter to 1/50, ISO to base, aperture to f/5.6 (if adjustable).
  3. Find open shade under a beach umbrella or palm tree for talking-head shots.
  4. For B-roll of waves/sand, expose for the highlights (tap the whitewater).
  5. Use a polarizing filter if shooting water — it cuts glare and reveals underwater detail.
  6. Shoot during the "shoulder hours" (10am–11am and 2pm–3pm) when the sun is slightly lower.

Post-Production Fixes

If you’ve already shot footage with blown highlights, all is not lost. In DaVinci Resolve or CapCut:

  • Use the "Highlight Recovery" tool to pull back clipped whites.
  • Add a subtle vignette to draw attention away from bright edges.
  • Lower the "Whites" slider and raise "Shadows" to compress dynamic range.
  • Apply a warm LUT to unify color temperature — harsh sun often casts a cool/blue tint.

Checklist: What to Pack for Sunny Shoots

  • [ ] ND Filters (ND8, ND16, ND32)
  • [ ] Circular Polarizer (CPL) for water/glass
  • [ ] Lens hood or matte box
  • [ ] Portable reflector (5-in-1 collapsible)
  • [ ] Microfiber cloth (salt spray + sun = smudges)
  • [ ] Shade umbrella or pop-up tent

Frequently Asked Questions

What ND filter strength do I need for beach filming?

Start with an ND16 for midday beach sun. If you’re shooting near water or white sand, bump up to ND32. For golden hour, drop to ND4 or remove it entirely.

Can I fix overexposed footage in editing?

Partially. Highlight recovery tools can salvage some detail, but once pixels are fully clipped (pure white), that data is gone forever. Prevention with ND filters is always superior.

Why does my GoPro footage look washed out in sunlight?

GoPros default to Auto Exposure, which prioritizes preventing overexposure by lifting shadows. Switch to Protune, set ISO Min/Max to 100, and add an ND filter to restore contrast.

Is it better to film early morning or late afternoon?

Absolutely. The "golden hours" (first and last 90 minutes of daylight) provide soft, directional light that requires zero ND filters and makes every camera look cinematic. Save harsh midday for B-roll only.
