The Complete Pre-Trip Camera Checklist for 2026

Gear, memory cards, settings, backups and the 20-minute night-before routine that has saved my footage — twice — refined over six years of travel filming.

The complete pre-trip camera checklist has five parts: charge everything, format every card in-camera, set your baseline video settings, pack a backup power and storage plan, and shoot a ten-second test clip before you leave. Do those five things the night before you fly and you've eliminated about 90% of the gear failures I've watched ruin other travelers' trips — and a few of my own early ones.

This is the exact routine I run before every trip, refined over six years of travel filming and one painful lesson per item. It works whether you carry a single phone or a bag of cameras. Print it, screenshot it, tattoo it — just don't skip the test clip at the end. For camera picks themselves, start with our best holiday video cameras 2026 roundup; this guide assumes the choosing is done and the packing has begun.

Key Takeaways

  • Format cards in-camera the night before — never assume last trip's card is empty.
  • Set a baseline: 4K, 25/30p, auto ISO capped, standard color — decide now, not at the attraction.
  • Two copies of everything: a backup plan that lives in a different bag than your camera.
  • Power is footage: a 10,000 mAh bank is as essential as the camera.
  • Shoot a test clip of your kitchen — it catches dead batteries, full cards and wrong settings.

The Gear List: What Actually Goes in the Bag

My carry-on camera kit, refined to the gram. Yours should be a subset, not a superset:

The Essentials

  • Camera (one — two only if the second is an action camera for water)
  • Two memory cards minimum, one in the camera, one in a different bag
  • Two batteries per camera, or one plus a power bank for USB-charging models
  • 10,000 mAh power bank and a short charging cable that lives in the camera bag
  • Lens cloth and a small blower — fingerprints and sea spray are inevitable
  • A zip-bag or dry bag for sudden rain and boat days

The Nice-to-Haves

  • Mini tripod or grip: sunset timelapses and group shots without strangers
  • Wireless mic: if you'll talk to camera, this matters more than any lens
  • ND filter for action cameras: keeps shutter speeds sane in bright sun
  • Silica gel packets: essential for humid destinations
Watch out: Batteries fly in carry-on, never checked luggage — that's an airline rule, not a preference. And a power bank over the airline's Wh limit can be confiscated at security; 10,000-20,000 mAh is safely within limits on virtually all carriers.

Memory Cards: Buy Right, Format Right

Card failures cause more lost holiday footage than broken cameras. Three rules:

Buy the right card. 4K video needs a card with a proper video speed class (V30 minimum for standard 4K, faster for high-bitrate modes), from a real brand, bought from a real retailer. Counterfeit cards are everywhere online. The full decoder ring is in our guide to choosing a memory card for 4K video.

Format in-camera, every trip. Not "delete all" — format. It rebuilds the card's file structure exactly how your camera wants it and wipes the lingering files from three trips ago that fill the card on day two. Format each card in the camera it'll be used in.

Never fill a card completely. Swap to your spare when a card hits roughly 80%. The last gigabytes are where corruption happens, and a fresh card on the final day of a trip is cheap insurance.

Camera Settings to Dial In Before You Fly

Decide your baseline at home, where there's no queue behind you. My defaults:

  • Resolution and frame rate: 4K at 25p or 30p (25p for Europe and other 50 Hz regions — it avoids light flicker). Set 50/60p or 120p as a quick-switch preset for slow motion.
  • Stabilization: on, at its standard level — max settings on some cameras crop heavily; test before you rely on it.
  • ISO: auto, with a cap you're comfortable with (I use 3200 on 1-inch-sensor cameras).
  • White balance: auto for most trips; lock it only for consistent scenes like night markets.
  • Color profile: standard unless you genuinely plan to grade — log footage you never edit just looks gray.
  • Date and time: sync every camera to local time at departure. Mismatched timestamps make merging phone and camera clips in the edit a special misery.
Pro tip: Save your baseline as a custom mode or preset if your camera supports it. After a day of experimenting with slow motion and log, one click returns you to sane defaults — instead of filming day three in last night's weird settings.

Backups: The Two-Copy Rule on the Road

Cards get lost, cameras get stolen, bags get left on ferries. The rule that has saved me twice: every clip exists in two places, and those two places travel separately.

My system is simple: each evening, I offload the day's footage from card to phone or a small SSD (most modern cameras transfer over USB-C or their apps), and I never format a card until its contents live somewhere else. On longer trips the second copy also goes to cloud storage whenever hotel Wi-Fi allows. The full workflow — including SSD picks and offline options — is in our guide to backing up travel photos and videos.

If that sounds like a lot, the minimum viable version is: don't delete anything from cards during the trip (cards are cheap; buy enough), and keep a spare card in a different bag from the camera. That's it — that's the floor.

The Night-Before Routine (In Order)

This takes 20 minutes. Do it the night before departure, every time:

  • 1. Charge everything: camera batteries, power bank, wireless mic, the camera itself if it charges via USB.
  • 2. Format every card in the camera it will live in.
  • 3. Check your baseline settings from the list above — resolution, frame rate, stabilization, ISO cap.
  • 4. Clean the lens and check the sensor for dust if your camera has interchangeable lenses.
  • 5. Update firmware if an update is pending — but never the night before if you can't test after.
  • 6. Shoot the test clip: ten seconds of your kitchen, with audio. Play it back. This catches dead mics, full cards, stuck gimbals and corrupted cards while fixes are still possible.
  • 7. Pack the bag with the camera accessible — the best travel footage happens at the airport and out the taxi window.

Run this routine and day one starts with filming, not troubleshooting. FAQ

What should I do to my camera before a trip?

Charge all batteries, format every memory card in-camera, set baseline video settings (4K, 25/30p, auto ISO capped), clean the lens, sync date and time, and shoot a short test clip with audio. The test clip catches most failures while you can still fix them.

Should I format my memory card before a trip?

Yes — format it in the camera it will be used in, the night before departure. Formatting rebuilds the card's file structure and clears old files completely. Only do it once the card's previous contents are backed up somewhere else.

How do I back up travel footage without a laptop?

Offload each day's clips to your phone or a small USB-C SSD using the camera's app or a card reader, and upload to cloud storage when Wi-Fi allows. The minimum safe habit: never delete from cards mid-trip, and keep a spare card in a different bag from the camera.

Can camera batteries go in checked luggage?

No — lithium camera batteries and power banks must travel in carry-on luggage under airline rules. Keep them in your cabin bag, and stay within your airline's power bank capacity limit (10,000-20,000 mAh is safely within virtually all limits).
