Dawid Millennium — Travel Videographer

I spent 2026 filming on three continents with everything from a 39 g wearable to an 8K 360 rig. Here's what I learned.

The state of travel cameras in 2026 comes down to four shifts: pocket gimbal cameras went mainstream, vertical video became the default format, AI took over editing, and flagship phones absorbed the bottom half of the camera market. After a year filming across three continents with everything from a thumb-sized wearable to an 8K 360 rig, here's my read on where travel video actually is — and what it means for your next purchase.

This isn't a press-release roundup. Every trend below is something I've felt in my bag, my workflow or my view counts this year. Some of it is genuinely exciting; some of it should make you hold onto your current camera longer. If you're shopping right now, pair this with our best holiday video cameras 2026 roundup — this piece is the "why" behind those rankings.

Key Takeaways

  • Pocket cinema is mainstream: the DJI Pocket 3 class replaced both camcorders and travel gimbals.
  • Vertical is the default: shoot for the phone screen first; horizontal is the derivative format now.
  • AI editing won: auto-cut, auto-reframe and music sync are how most travel videos get made.
  • Phones ate the entry level: below ~$300, dedicated cameras barely make sense anymore.
  • What survives: waterproofing, real stabilization and sensors bigger than a phone's.

Trend 1: Pocket Cinema Went Mainstream

Two years ago the DJI Osmo Pocket 3 was a creator niche. In 2026 it's the camera I see most at tourist spots — more than GoPros, more than mirrorless rigs. The formula won because it solved travel video's oldest problem: smooth footage without a rig. A 1-inch sensor on a mechanical gimbal, in a jacket pocket, starting in seconds. My DJI Osmo Pocket 3 review predicted this; I underestimated how completely.

The ripple effects are everywhere. Competitors now build around the category instead of pretending it doesn't exist, the used market is flooded with phone gimbals nobody wants anymore, and "pocket cinema" roundups like our pocket cinema cameras 2026 roundup have gone from novelty to necessity. The traditional camcorder is effectively dead for travelers — killed not by phones, but by gimbals with good sensors.

Trend 2: Vertical Video Became the Default

I resisted this one for years. The data won. The travel clips I post vertically get multiples of the reach of the same edit horizontal, and every platform's algorithm now assumes a phone screen. In 2026 I shoot with the vertical edit in mind from the first frame — even when the final deliverable is a YouTube video.

Camera makers noticed. The Pocket 3's rotating screen flips to vertical natively; action cameras now shoot near-square sensors you can crop either way; 360 cameras make the whole debate moot by letting you export both from one clip. If your camera workflow still treats vertical as an afterthought, our vertical video settings guide for travel has the exact setups I use per camera.

Pro tip: Shoot horizontal masters with headroom when you can, then crop vertical in the edit — you get both formats from one take. On cameras with square-ish sensors (or any 360 camera), frame loose and decide the aspect ratio after the trip.

Trend 3: AI Editing Took Over the Workflow

The biggest change in my travel video life this year wasn't a camera — it was that I stopped editing most clips myself. Auto-highlight selection, beat-synced cuts, one-tap reframing of 360 footage, voice-controlled rough cuts on the plane: the AI features that felt like gimmicks in 2024 are now simply how travel videos get made. Insta360's and DJI's apps turn a day of clips into a watchable minute before dinner.

The craft hasn't died — it's moved upstream. What matters now is shooting enough good raw material and knowing which moments the algorithm can't invent: the establishing shot, the reaction, the transition. Even frame-rate choices have become editing decisions the software can exploit — our deep dive on 24fps vs 120fps and AI music sync in holiday edits shows how slow-motion capture feeds these tools. And for the apps themselves, our best editing apps for holiday footage guide ranks what actually saves time.

Watch out: Auto-edits all converge on the same look — same transitions, same beat drops, same speed ramps. If you want your video to be recognizable as yours, let AI build the rough cut, then spend ten minutes replacing two or three of its choices with your own.

Trend 4: The Smartphone Convergence

The uncomfortable truth I keep repeating to readers: a flagship phone in 2026 shoots travel video that would have embarrassed dedicated cameras five years ago. The iPhone 16 Pro's 4K 120 slow motion, Log recording and stabilization cover most casual travelers completely — my iPhone 16 Pro video review concluded that many people should simply not buy a camera, and I stand by it.

The result is a barbell market. Below roughly $300, dedicated cameras have nearly evaporated — why would they exist? What survives above the phone does so on things phones physically can't do: waterproofing without a case (action cameras), mechanical stabilization (gimbal cameras), shoot-everything capture (360), and sensors large enough for real low light. The middle — the $400 point-and-shoot — is the segment I expect to keep shrinking through 2027.

What This Means for Your Next Camera (and 2027)

Practical advice from all four trends. First, buy above the phone or not at all: if a camera can't beat your phone on stabilization, waterproofing or low light, save the money. Second, treat the app ecosystem as part of the purchase — in 2026 you're buying half a camera and half an editing pipeline, and DJI and Insta360 currently lead that second half. Third, don't chase resolution; 4K with great stabilization and good audio beats 8K you can't watch smoothly.

My predictions for 2027: vertical-native sensors in mainstream cameras, AI editing moving on-device in real time (your camera rough-cutting as you shoot), and at least one major brand attempting a phone-gimbal hybrid. I'd also bet on 360 capture getting smaller and cheaper until it's a standard second camera. Whatever launches, we'll test it the way we always do — on real trips, in real weather, for our City Through the Lens series. FAQ

What is the biggest travel camera trend of 2026?

Pocket gimbal cameras going mainstream. The DJI Osmo Pocket 3 class — a large sensor on a mechanical gimbal in a jacket pocket — replaced both travel camcorders and phone gimbals, and is now the most common dedicated camera seen at tourist spots.

Should I shoot vertical or horizontal travel video in 2026?

Plan for vertical first — phone-screen platforms dominate reach — but shoot horizontal masters with headroom (or a 360/square sensor) so one take yields both formats. Vertical is now the default; horizontal is the derivative edit.

Is it still worth buying a travel camera, or is a phone enough?

A flagship phone like the iPhone 16 Pro covers most casual travelers. Buy a dedicated camera only for what phones can't do: waterproofing without a case, mechanical gimbal stabilization, 360 capture, or genuinely better low light. Below roughly $300, dedicated cameras rarely make sense.

Will AI replace video editing for travel videos?

For most travelers it already has — auto-highlight, beat-synced cuts and one-tap reframing produce watchable videos in minutes. The remaining craft is upstream: capturing strong raw moments, then personalizing the AI's rough cut so your video doesn't look like everyone else's.
