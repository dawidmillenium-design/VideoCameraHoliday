What Water Does to Cameras & Color

Water attacks your footage before it ever threatens your hardware. On my first snorkel trip to the Red Sea I shot two hours of stunning reefs that all came back looking like they'd been filmed through a bottle of mouthwash. Here's why:

  • Color loss with depth: Water eats the warm end of the spectrum first. Red is largely gone by 5m, orange by 10m, yellow by 20m. Without correction, everything below the shallows is a flat blue-green wash and coral looks like concrete.
  • Light loss: Even tropical sun drops off fast. By 10m you're working with a fraction of surface light, which pushes small sensors toward noisy, muddy footage. Morning dives in flat light beat afternoon dives in chop.
  • Reduced sharpness and contrast: Suspended particles ("marine snow") and the water itself soften detail. Every meter between lens and subject costs you clarity — this is why pros say get close, then get closer.
  • Refraction: Everything looks about 25% closer and bigger underwater through a flat port, which takes some getting used to when framing.
  • Pressure: Depth ratings are real limits. Buttons get harder to press under pressure, seals compress, and any pre-existing damage to an O-ring will find you at 15m.
Warning: Never exceed your camera or housing's rated depth, and never assume a scratched or sandy O-ring will seal. Inspect the O-ring before every single water session, clean it with a lint-free cloth, and make sure not one hair or grain of sand crosses the seal when you close the door.

Underwater Shooting Techniques

Get close. Then halve the distance. The single biggest upgrade to underwater footage is reducing the water column between lens and subject. Good buoyancy and a slow approach beat any camera upgrade. With a wide action-cam lens, your subject should fill a third of the frame from arm's length.

Red filters below 5m. A red/magenta filter restores the warm colors the water steals, and it works from about 5m down to 20m in blue water. Above 3-4m it makes everything pink, so snorkelers near the surface should skip it and use the camera's underwater white balance mode instead. Filters snap on and off — film the shallows clean, filter up for the drop-off.

Shoot with the sun behind you. Down there you're the drone: position yourself so sunlight hits the reef over your shoulder. Silhouettes of turtles and divers against the surface ("snell's window" shots) are the exception — those are gorgeous and worth practising.

Slow everything down. Slow kicks, slow pans, slow ascents. Underwater footage with fast movement is nauseating, and stirring up sand with your fins backscatters particles into every shot after it. Shoot 4K/60 so you can slow clips by 40% in the edit.

Pro tip: Do a 30-second test clip at the start of every session and check it on the surface: look for fog inside the housing, a hair across the seal, and correct white balance. Thirty seconds of checking has saved me entire dive days.

Housing, Anti-Fog & Rinse Care

  • Anti-fog inserts are mandatory in the tropics. Warm humid air trapped in a sealed housing condenses on the coldest surface — the inside of the lens port. Drop a fresh anti-fog insert in before every session and replace or dry them daily. No insert, no footage: I've watched a whole boat of GoPros fog up within ten minutes of splash.
  • Seal the housing in air-conditioning, not on the beach. Trap cool, dry air inside the housing. Closing it in humid beach air imports the moisture that fogs it later.
  • Rinse after every saltwater session. Soak the camera/housing in fresh water for 10-20 minutes and work every button while submerged to flush salt out of the mechanisms. Salt crystals left in button shafts are tomorrow's leak.
  • Never leave gear in direct sun to dry. Heat + a sealed housing = pressure and fog. Dry in shade with the door open once you're away from water.
  • O-ring discipline: inspect, clean, and lightly grease silicone O-rings per manufacturer guidance. Replace yearly or at the first nick.
Warning: Freshwater rinse tanks on dive boats are for cameras only after the "mask bucket" rule — never dunk your camera in the shared mask/defog bucket, and ask the crew which tank is which. Soap and defogger residue smears lens ports badly.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a dive housing for snorkeling?

Usually not. The DJI Osmo Action 5 Pro is natively waterproof to 20m and the GoPro Hero 13 Black to 10m, both deeper than any snorkeler free-dives. Housings become necessary for scuba below 10m, or if you want to attach red filters that require the housing mount.

Why does my underwater footage look all blue and green?

Water absorbs warm colors with depth: red disappears by about 5m, orange by 10m, yellow by 20m. Below 5m, use a red filter (in blue water) or the camera's underwater color mode to restore warmth, and get as close to your subject as possible to reduce the water column.

How do I stop my underwater housing fogging up?

Use a fresh anti-fog insert inside the housing for every session, seal the housing in cool dry (air-conditioned) air rather than humid beach air, and avoid opening it on the boat. If fog appears mid-dive, the footage is unrecoverable — prevention is the only fix.

How should I clean my camera after saltwater use?

Rinse or soak it in fresh water for 10-20 minutes the same day, pressing every button underwater to flush salt from the mechanisms. Dry it in shade, open port doors only once fully dry and away from water, and inspect O-rings and seals for sand or salt crystals before the next use.
