Dawid Millennium — Travel Videographer

From Scottish downpours to Vietnamese monsoons, I've learned exactly where the line is between great rain footage and a dead camera.

🕐 Last updated: August 25, 2026

To film in the rain on holiday: know your camera's actual limits (action cameras like the GoPro Hero 13 are waterproof to 10m; mirrorless cameras are only weather-sealed, never waterproof), add a rain cover or dry bag for real protection, fight lens fog with anti-fog inserts and temperature management, and follow a 15-minute post-rain care routine every single time. Rain makes some of the most beautiful travel footage possible — reflective streets, moody skies, empty landmarks — as long as your gear survives it.

I've filmed through a Scottish downpour, Vietnam's monsoon season, and an unexpected hailstorm in the Dolomites. The footage from those days is some of the best I've ever shot. I've also watched a friend's unsealed camera die from what he called "just a drizzle." The difference between those outcomes is everything below.

Key Takeaways

  • Waterproof and weather-sealed are not the same thing — know which one your camera actually is.
  • A $15 rain cover protects a $1,500 camera better than hope and a plastic bag.
  • Fogged lenses come from temperature swings, not rain itself — acclimate gear gradually.
  • After rain: dry the outside, open nothing until it's dry, then air it out with silica gel overnight.
  • Rain footage is worth the hassle — wet streets and storm light are cinematic gold.

Waterproof vs Weather-Sealed: Know Your Actual Limits

Manufacturers love vague weather language, so let's be precise:

Protection LevelWhat It MeansExamples
Waterproof (rated)Submersible to a stated depth without any caseGoPro Hero 13 Black (10m), DJI Osmo Action 5 Pro (20m), Insta360 X5 (15m)
Weather-sealedGaskets resist rain and splashes — NOT submersion, and warranties often exclude water damageMost mid/high-end mirrorless bodies and L-mount/E-mount weather-sealed lenses
No ratingAny water is a gambleDJI Osmo Pocket 3, Sony ZV-1 II, most compact cameras, phones without IP68

The trap: weather sealing is a resistor, not a shield. It handles drizzle and splashes, but seals age, gaskets compress, and a driven rain finds the microphone holes and battery doors. And critically, water damage is excluded from most warranties — "weather-sealed" is a marketing promise, not an insurance policy. My GoPro Hero 13 review and DJI Action 5 Pro review cover exactly how far you can push genuinely waterproof cameras; for everything else, add protection. For beach and water-heavy trips, also see our waterproof cameras guide.

Rain Protection Gear That Actually Works

1. Rain Covers

A proper camera rain cover — a clear-backed sleeve with drawstrings around the lens and your hands — costs around $10–40 and lets you keep shooting normally in moderate rain. Op/Tech and Think Tank make good ones; even the disposable emergency covers work. For mirrorless setups, this is the single best rain purchase. Practice putting it on at home; fiddling with drawstrings in a downpour with cold hands is miserable.

2. Dry Bags and the Ziplock Method

For transport — boat trips, kayaking, monsoon transit days — a roll-top dry bag (5–10L) keeps gear genuinely dry even in a soaking. Inside the bag, I wrap the camera in a zip-lock bag with a silica gel packet: double protection for about a dollar. When shooting pauses, the camera goes in the bag immediately, not "after this one more shot."

3. Lens Cloth Discipline

Pack 3–5 microfiber cloths in a waterproof pouch, plus a lens pen. The routine: one cloth stays dry for the front element only, one for the body. A single raindrop on the lens at f/8 renders as a giant blurry blob across your entire clip — in rain, I check and wipe the front element between almost every shot.

Tip: Attach a lens hood before rain starts. It's not just for sun — a hood keeps the front element remarkably drier in anything but horizontal rain, and UV filters (a clear sacrificial piece of glass) make wiping safer and faster.

Anti-Fog: The Problem Nobody Warns You About

Here's what actually ruins rainy-day footage: not water on the camera, but fog inside the lens. It happens when cold gear meets warm humid air — stepping from an air-conditioned bus into monsoon heat, or bringing a rain-chilled camera into a warm café. The moisture condenses inside the optics, and no amount of wiping helps.

Prevention: seal the camera in a zip-lock bag before moving between temperature zones, and let it acclimate inside the bag for 15–30 minutes. The condensation forms on the bag, not your sensor. In tropical climates, I carry anti-fog inserts (originally made for GoPro housings — they work in camera bags too) and keep a handful of rechargeable silica gel packets in every compartment. If the lens does fog: stop filming, power off, and wait it out in a dry place. Never use heat — a hairdryer can damage coatings and push moisture deeper.

The Post-Rain Care Routine (15 Minutes, Non-Negotiable)

  1. Wipe the entire exterior dry with a microfiber cloth, paying attention to seams, dials, and port doors.
  2. Don't open anything yet. No battery doors, no card slots, no lens changes until the camera is fully dry — opening a wet camera invites water inside.
  3. Remove the lens outdoors-style check: once dry, remove the lens and inspect the mount and sensor area with a light. If you see any moisture, stop and move to step 4 immediately.
  4. Overnight airing: camera body (battery and card out, doors open) and lens go into a sealed container or dry bag with silica gel packets overnight. Silica is reusable — recharge the packets on a radiator or in the sun.
  5. Check the ports before charging: USB-C ports must be bone dry before you plug anything in. Corrosion from charging a wet port kills cameras weeks later.
Warning: Never use rice. It's a myth for modern electronics — rice dust gets into ports and mechanisms, and it absorbs moisture far slower than silica gel. If your camera took a real soaking, power it off, pull the battery, and get it to a repair shop rather than hoping.

Monsoon Field Notes: Why Rain Days Are Worth It

Two weeks of monsoon season in Hoi An taught me that rain days produce the footage everyone remembers. Streets turn to mirrors. Markets glow under awnings. Tourist crowds vanish, and you get empty ancient towns in moody light. My routine there: waterproof Insta360 X5 on a short grip for street-level shots in active rain (fully rated, zero anxiety), mirrorless under a rain cover for golden-hour breaks between showers, everything in dry bags the moment the sky opened again.

The pragmatic rules I follow: shoot the rain, don't hike in it ( slippery trails and cameras don't mix); keep a "rain kit" pouch — cover, cloths, silica, zip-locks — permanently packed so there's no decision to make; and when the rain stops, move fast, because post-storm light is the best light there is. These wet-weather city walks became some of my favorite episodes of City Through the Lens. And if you're shooting on hard travel days, pair this with gimbal-free stabilization — wet hands and a gimbal are a bad combination.

Common Rain Filming Mistakes

  • Trusting weather sealing as waterproofing. It's splash resistance, not a dive rating. Add a cover.
  • Changing lenses in the rain. One gust and your sensor is wet. Change lenses under cover or indoors only.
  • Wiping a fogged inner lens forever. Internal fog can't be wiped away — prevent it with acclimation bags, or wait it out.
  • Opening port doors while wet. Dry the camera fully before touching battery or card slots.
  • Charging through a wet USB port. Corrosion from this kills cameras slowly, long after the trip.
  • Skipping the shot. The biggest mistake: packing up when rain starts. Protect the gear, then keep filming — that's where the magic is.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a weather-sealed camera safe in the rain?

Weather sealing resists rain and splashes but is not waterproofing, and water damage is typically excluded from warranties. Light drizzle is usually fine on a sealed body with a sealed lens, but for sustained rain add a rain cover, and never submerge a weather-sealed camera.

Can I use a GoPro in heavy rain without a case?

Yes. Cameras like the GoPro Hero 13 Black are waterproof to 10 meters without any housing, so rain, waterfalls, and boat spray are no issue. Just make sure all port doors are fully closed and dry the camera before opening anything or charging.

How do I stop my camera lens from fogging in humid weather?

Fog comes from temperature swings, not rain. Seal the camera in a zip-lock bag before moving between air-conditioned and hot humid environments, and let it acclimate inside the bag for 15-30 minutes. Keep silica gel packets in your bag and never use direct heat on a fogged lens.

What should I do if my camera gets soaked?

Power it off immediately, remove the battery if possible, dry the exterior, and place it in a sealed container with silica gel overnight with doors open. Do not charge through a wet USB port, do not use rice or a hairdryer, and seek a repair shop for anything beyond light rain exposure.
