    How to Film in Harsh Sunlight (Without Ruining Your Footage)

    Midday sun, harsh shadows and beach glare — the exposure strategy, ND filter logic and positioning tricks I use on real trips.

    📅 August 25, 2026 ⏱️ 9 min read ✅ Updated: August 25, 2026
    1. Home
    2. How-To
    3. Film in Harsh Sunlight
    🎬

    Dawid Millennium — Travel Videographer

    Shot through tropical noon on beaches across Southeast Asia and the Med — this is what survived the edit.

    🕐 Last updated: August 25, 2026

    📋 In This Guide

    • ND Filters: The Beach Essential

    You can absolutely film great travel video in harsh midday sun — the trick is to stop fighting the light and start managing it. Lock your shutter speed to double your frame rate, use an ND filter or a faster shutter to control exposure, and position subjects so the sun works as a backlight or edge light instead of blasting straight into faces. I've shot through dozens of brutal 1 p.m. sessions on beaches from Bali to the Algarve, and the footage that survived always followed the same handful of rules.

    On a white-sand beach in Nusa Penida last July, I watched a family next to me give up filming entirely at noon because "everything looks terrible." Their phone was auto-exposing for the sky, turning their kids into silhouettes with raccoon-eye shadows. Ten minutes of settings changes would have fixed it. This guide is those ten minutes.

    ⚡ Key Takeaways

    • Lock shutter speed to roughly double your frame rate (1/50 for 25fps, 1/60 for 30fps) and control brightness with ND filters, not shutter.
    • Expose for faces, not the sky — a slightly clipped sky is fine, a blacked-out face is not.
    • Shoot with the sun behind or to the side of your subject at midday, never behind the camera.
    • An ND16 handles most beach noon conditions; polarizers help with sea glare but cost you light.
    • Heat kills cameras faster than light does — shade the body between takes.

    Why Midday Sun Is So Hard on Cameras

    At noon in the tropics, the dynamic range of a beach scene is enormous — blazing white sand, deep blue sky, and faces shaded by brows and noses all in one frame. Your camera's sensor can hold maybe 10-12 stops of that; the scene has far more. Auto exposure splits the difference and gives you the worst of both: a washed-out sky and muddy faces.

    The second problem is shadow direction. Overhead sun carves shadows straight down, which turns eye sockets into caves and adds five years to anyone you film. I learned this shooting a cooking segment with a street vendor in Marrakech at 1 p.m. — lovely man, terrible footage. We re-shot at 5 p.m. and he looked like a different person.

    The third problem is heat. Direct sun on a black camera body pushes internal temperatures up fast, and small cameras — action cams especially — start throttling or shutting down right when the light is strongest. More on that below.

    A Simple Midday Exposure Strategy

    Here's the exact sequence I run through every time I step into harsh sun. It takes about 30 seconds once it's a habit:

    • Step 1 — Lock your frame rate and shutter. Set 4K/30fps and shutter at 1/60 (or 25fps at 1/50 in PAL countries). This keeps motion blur natural instead of that stuttery, Saving-Private-Ryan look you get from a 1/2000 auto shutter.
    • Step 2 — Set ISO to its base value. Usually ISO 100. In full sun you'll have light to spare, so there's no reason to let the camera add gain.
    • Step 3 — Cut the light with an ND filter. With shutter locked, the only honest way to expose correctly is to remove light before it hits the sensor. Screw on an ND and adjust until the meter sits at zero.
    • Step 4 — Expose for the face, then check the sky. Point at your subject's face, lock exposure there, and accept that the sky may clip a little. Viewers forgive blown highlights; they don't forgive a subject they can't see.
    • Step 5 — Use zebras or the histogram if you have them. I set zebras to around 95-100% and only worry when large areas of skin — not sky — start striping.
    Field tip: If your camera has no ND thread and no manual mode (many phones), tap to expose on the brightest part of your subject's face, then drag the exposure slider down a touch before hitting record. On an iPhone, lock AE/AF with a long press so it doesn't hunt mid-shot.

    ND Filters: The Beach Essential

    If you buy one accessory for sunny travel filming, make it a neutral density filter. It's sunglasses for your lens, and it's the only tool that lets you keep cinematic shutter speeds at noon.

    Which strength for which light

    After two beach-heavy trips shooting with the GoPro Hero 13 Black and the DJI Osmo Pocket 3, here's where I've landed:

    ConditionsTypical ND strengthNotes from the field
    Overcast / open shadeND4-ND8Often not needed at all; check shutter first
    Bright sun, city streetsND8-ND16ND16 is my default walking-around filter
    Midday beach, snow, waterND16-ND32Reflective surfaces add a stop or more of light
    Tropical noon on white sandND32The Nusa Penida scenario — don't leave it in the hotel

    A small caveat on polarizers: they cut sea glare beautifully and make water look like glass, but they eat roughly two stops of light and can make skies uneven on wide action-cam lenses. On the GoPro I skip the polarizer and use plain NDs.

    Warning: Cheap no-name ND filters often add a green or magenta cast that's miserable to fix in the edit, especially on skin. Buy from a known brand — a filter set usually runs around $20-$60 depending on the camera — and do a quick white-balance check after screwing it on.

    Work With Shadows, Not Against Them

    Settings get you a correctly exposed disaster if the angle is wrong. My midday rules after six years of trial and error:

    Sun behind or beside the subject

    Put the sun at your subject's back or at a 45-degree angle. You get a rim of light on hair and shoulders, the face falls into soft shade, and the footage suddenly looks deliberate instead of accidental. If the face goes too dark, use your exposure compensation — or a white t-shirt, hotel towel, or the sandy ground itself as a free bounce.

    Find "open shade"

    The single best midday filming spot is the shadow edge of a building, umbrella, or palm tree, with the subject just inside the shade facing the bright scene. The bright background acts like a giant softbox. I filmed an entire Lisbon food-tour segment under café awnings this way at 2 p.m. and nobody could tell it was midday.

    Embrace hard shadows as graphics

    Palm shadows on sand, stripes through market awnings, a traveler's long shadow stretching across a plaza — midday light is terrible for faces but gorgeous for graphic b-roll. When I can't make people look good at noon, I film textures and shadows instead and save the people shots for later. That's exactly the kind of scheduling logic we use across our City Through the Lens series, where local videographers plan whole shooting days around their city's light.

    Protecting Gear (and Yourself) in the Heat

    Harsh sun and heat are the same problem. On a boat trip in Thailand my action cam shut down twice in an hour until I started doing three things: keeping it in a shaded bag between takes, leaving a small airflow gap rather than sealing it in a case while it cooled, and dropping to 1080p for long static shots where 4K wasn't needed. The footage difference was invisible; the temperature difference was not.

    Warning: Never leave a camera on a car dashboard, boat deck, or dark bag in direct sun — internal temps can climb far past the operating rating in minutes, and batteries hate it. If the body is painful to hold, it's past time to stop recording. Wet-weather protection is a different beast; for that, see our guide on filming in the rain and protecting your gear.

    And the boring truth: the best midday strategy is often not filming at noon at all. I shoot b-roll and shadow graphics at midday, nap through the worst of it, and save people shots for the golden-hour window covered in our guide to filming sunsets and golden hour. Once the light softens, the stabilization techniques in our gimbal-free stabilization guide will get you the rest of the way to footage that looks like you planned the whole day — because now you did.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    What ND filter do I need for a sunny beach?

    For tropical midday sun on sand or water, ND32 is the reliable choice; ND16 covers bright but less reflective city scenes. The goal is simply to bring your shutter speed back down to double your frame rate (1/60 for 30fps), so carry both and swap as the light changes.

    Can I film in harsh sunlight without an ND filter?

    Yes, with compromises. Let the shutter speed rise (the camera will do this automatically) and accept slightly stuttery motion, or stop the aperture down on cameras that allow it. For quick clips the results are fine; for anything cinematic, a cheap ND set is worth the roughly $20-$60.

    Why do faces look so dark in my sunny beach videos?

    Your camera is auto-exposing for the bright sky and sand, so the face — the darkest thing in frame — gets crushed. Tap or meter on the face, lock that exposure, and let the sky brighten. Positioning the sun behind your subject also evens things out dramatically.

    Does a polarizer replace an ND filter for travel video?

    No. A polarizer cuts reflections and glare from water and glass but only reduces about two stops of light and can create patchy skies on very wide lenses. For video shutter control you still want ND filters; treat a polarizer as an optional extra for seascapes.
