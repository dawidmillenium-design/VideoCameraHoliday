Dawid Millennium — Travel Videographer

I've vlogged across 30+ countries since 2019 and deleted more bad footage than most people ever shoot — these are the rules that fixed it.

🕐 Last updated: August 25, 2026

  • Rules 4–5: Light and Timing
  • Rules 6–7: Sequences and Sound
  • Rules 8–10: People, Backups, Finishing

  • FAQ

The difference between amateur and professional-looking holiday video isn't the camera — it's shot discipline. Film short, intentional clips (10 seconds each), chase golden-hour light, think in sequences of wide/medium/close shots, capture usable audio, and film people with respect. These 10 rules come from six years of travel vlogging across more than 30 countries, and every single one of them was learned by ruining footage first. Start with our cheat sheet for travel video camera settings for beginners.

My earliest travel videos from 2019 are unwatchable: five-minute pans across everything, shouting over wind noise, backs of heads. My recent footage gets comments asking what cinema camera I use. It's the same mid-range gear. What changed is the discipline below — and none of it costs a cent.

Key Takeaways

  • Apply the 10-second rule: every shot is one idea, held for about 10 seconds, never a wandering pan.
  • Plan your day around golden hour — the hour after sunrise and before sunset does more for your footage than any camera upgrade.
  • Shoot sequences (wide, medium, close) of every scene so the edit builds itself.
  • Audio is half the video: get close, shield the mic from wind, and record 30 seconds of ambient sound everywhere.
  • Film people like guests in their home: ask, smile, show them the clip, and back off when it's not welcome.

Rules 1–3: Shot Discipline

Rule 1: The 10-Second Rule

Before pressing record, decide what the shot is about. Then hold that one idea for roughly 10 seconds and stop. Not a 90-second wander around the plaza — 10 seconds of the church facade, 10 seconds of the fountain, 10 seconds of the street musician. In Marrakech's medina I counted my clips: the usable ones averaged 8–12 seconds. The clips over a minute? I never used a single one. Short clips are easier to stabilize (see our gimbal-free stabilization techniques), faster to edit, and force you to think about each frame.

Rule 2: Hold the Shot — Then Hold It Longer

The most common amateur tell is "firehosing": constantly moving the camera like you're spraying a garden. Pick a composition, press record, and stay completely still for 3 seconds before and after the action. Those handles give you room to cut and let viewers actually see the scene. If movement is needed, move once, slowly, with intent — a single pan or a single push-in, never both.

Rule 3: One Idea Per Shot

A shot of the market is noise. A shot of the vendor's hands wrapping spices is a story. Every clip should answer "what am I showing?" in one phrase. If you can't name it, don't record it — you'll just spend your evening deleting it anyway. This is also the mindset behind my City Through the Lens series: fewer shots, each one earning its place.

Rules 4–5: Light and Timing

Rule 4: Build Your Day Around Golden Hour

The hour after sunrise and the hour before sunset give you warm, directional light that makes even a phone camera look cinematic. Harsh midday sun creates raccoon-eye shadows and blown highlights that no editing can rescue. My travel routine: sunrise shoot, long lazy lunch during harsh light, explore and scout in the afternoon, then film the "hero shots" in the last 90 minutes of daylight. In Santorini, the same alley I filmed at noon looked flat and ugly; at 7pm it looked like a film set. Check sunrise/sunset times for your destination and put them in your calendar like flights.

Tip: Don't pack up when the sun sets. The 20–30 minutes of "blue hour" afterwards — deep blue sky, city lights switched on — is the single most beautiful light for urban travel footage. Cameras with bigger sensors, like the DJI Osmo Pocket 3 with its 1-inch sensor, really earn their keep here.

Rule 5: Make Midday and Bad Weather Work For You

You can't always wait for golden hour — tours leave at 11am, kids nap at 2pm. In harsh light, shoot in open shade (arcades, narrow alleys, under awnings), put the sun behind your subject and expose for faces, or lean into hard contrast for graphic street shots. Overcast days are secretly great: the whole sky becomes a giant softbox, perfect for people and food. Rain makes streets reflective and moody — just protect your gear properly (more on that in our rain filming guide).

Rules 6–7: Sequences and Sound

Rule 6: Think in Sequences — Wide, Medium, Close

Professionals don't film scenes; they film sequences. For every location, capture three layers: a wide establishing shot (where are we?), a medium shot (what's happening?), and close-ups (details — hands, food, textures, faces). Three to five clips per sequence. In the edit, cutting wide → medium → close creates instant visual rhythm. I film every meal this way: wide of the restaurant, medium of the dish arriving, close-up of the first bite. Thirty seconds of footage, and it cuts together like a travel show.

Rule 7: Audio Is Half the Video

Viewers forgive shaky video; they abandon bad audio in seconds. Built-in camera mics are usable only when you're close (within an arm's length) and shielded from wind. Cup your hand or body to block the breeze, or use a cheap foam/furry windscreen — dead cats are the best money-per-quality upgrade in travel video. And record 30 seconds of clean ambient sound at every location: market chatter, waves, church bells. Layering real ambience under your edit is what makes footage feel like you're there. For vlogging, a compact wireless mic clipped to your collar beats any camera's built-in option; my GoPro Hero 13 review covers how action cams handle wind noise in the real world.

Rules 8–10: People, Backups, and Finishing

Rule 8: Film People Like a Guest

You're visiting someone's home, workplace, and daily life. Make eye contact, smile, raise the camera slightly with a questioning look — most people will nod or wave you off, and both answers are fine. Never film children without a parent's clear permission. Show vendors the clip you shot of them; that 10 seconds of connection has gotten me better footage (and better food tips) than any zoom lens. In some countries and religious sites, filming restrictions are legal matters, not etiquette — check signage and ask.

Rule 9: Back Up Every Night, No Exceptions

The rule I enforce after losing a full day in Croatia to a corrupted card: every evening, footage goes to a second device. It takes 10 minutes before bed and means a stolen bag or dead camera costs you a day of memories, not a whole trip. The full system is in how to back up travel footage without a laptop.

Rule 10: Edit It, or It Never Happened

Unedited footage is a diary you never open. A 90-second trip video — sequences cut to music, 10-second clips trimmed to their best 3 — gets watched, shared, and rewatched for decades. You don't need a laptop or a weekend; 30 minutes on your phone on the flight home is enough. Our phone and tablet editing workflow is built exactly for that flight home.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Filming everything in one endless clip. You'll never edit it. Short intentional shots or nothing.
  • Zooming instead of moving. Digital zoom destroys quality; walk closer or crop later.
  • Chasing only landmarks. The gelato melting, the ticket stub, your travel partner laughing — that's the footage you'll cry at in 20 years.
  • Ignoring wind. Ten seconds of ruined audio per clip adds up to an unusable video.
  • Never appearing in your own videos. Hand the camera over, set it down, be in your memories.

Dealing with Crowds

Sometimes you can't avoid them, but often you can. We break down 7 pro techniques to film travel videos without crowds, from timing your shoots to lens choices.

Warning: Don't let filming consume the trip. Two focused shooting windows a day (golden hours) plus spontaneous 10-second clips is plenty. If you experience the whole holiday through a screen, you've made a documentary about a trip you didn't take.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the 10-second rule for travel video?

Film every shot as a single idea held for about 10 seconds, then stop recording. Short, intentional clips are steadier, far easier to edit, and force you to compose deliberately instead of panning aimlessly across a scene.

How do I film people respectfully when traveling?

Make eye contact, smile, and raise the camera with a questioning gesture before filming strangers. Accept a no gracefully, never film children without a parent's permission, and show people the clip afterward. Check local laws and signage at religious or sensitive sites.

What is the best time of day to film holiday videos?

Golden hour — the hour after sunrise and before sunset — gives warm, directional light that flatters any camera. Stay out for blue hour just after sunset for city scenes. In harsh midday sun, work in open shade or use backlight instead.

Do I need an expensive camera for good travel videos?

No. Shot discipline, good light, sequence thinking, and clean audio matter far more than gear. A phone used with these rules beats a $2,000 camera used carelessly — upgrade only when technique is no longer your bottleneck.
