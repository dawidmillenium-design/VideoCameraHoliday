⚡ Quick Answer

The secret isn't expensive gear; it's timing. Arrive at popular spots (Trevi Fountain, Shibuya Crossing) by 6:00 AM. Use a wide-angle lens pointed upwards to exclude ground-level crowds. If you must shoot mid-day, use timelapses to blur people out or keep the camera moving so subjects don't linger in frame.

Key Takeaways

  • Wake Up Early: 6:00 AM to 7:30 AM is the only window for truly empty shots in major cities.
  • Look Up: Crowds are at eye level. Pointing your camera slightly up isolates architecture against the sky.
  • Wide Angle: A 16mm lens lets you get close to a subject, blurring background distractions.
  • Movement: A walking shot (gimbal) pushes through crowds; a static shot gets blocked by them.

Technique 1: The Magic Hour Rule

Tourists operate on a 9-to-5 schedule. They arrive at 10 AM and leave by 5 PM. To beat them, you must be there when they are asleep. I filmed the Colosseum at 6:15 AM and had it entirely to myself. By 9:00 AM, it was impassable.

Strategy: Scout your location on Google Maps the night before. Calculate travel time. Be set up and recording 15 minutes before sunrise.

Technique 2: Lens Choice Matters

Using a telephoto lens (zoom) compresses the background, making distant crowds look denser than they are. Instead, use a wide-angle lens (16mm-24mm).

Why? Wide angles exaggerate distance. Objects close to the lens look huge; objects 10 meters away look tiny. Get close to your subject (a statue, a building detail) and the crowd behind them will shrink into insignificance.

Pro Tip: Pair a wide-angle lens with a low aperture (f/2.8). This blurs the background further, turning distracting people into soft, unrecognizable blobs. See our guide on Best Wide-Angle Lenses for Travel →

Technique 3: Change Your Angle

Everyone shoots from eye level (5-6 feet high). This is exactly where the crowd is. Break the pattern:

  • Go Low: Put your camera on a mini tripod near the ground. The foreground is clear, and people's legs become a blurry river of motion.
  • Go High: Find a balcony, roof, or second-story window. Shooting down eliminates the horizon line where crowds gather.

Technique 4: Keep Moving

A static camera invites people to walk in front of it. A moving camera pushes through them. Use a gimbal to walk smoothly towards your subject. People naturally dodge a moving object, creating a temporary path for you.

Gear Recommendation: A compact gimbal like the DJI RS3 Mini or Zhiyun Weebill → is essential for this technique.

Technique 5: The Timelapse Trick

If you can't beat them, blur them. Set up your camera on a sturdy tripod and shoot a timelapse (1 frame every 2 seconds). In the final video, fast-moving people will vanish or become ghostly streaks, leaving the static architecture sharp and clear.

⚠️ Legal Note: Tripods are banned in many busy areas (Subways, some museums). Check local laws or use a clamp mount on a railing instead. See our NYC Filming Laws Guide →

Essential Gear for Crowd Control

Having the right tools makes these techniques easier. Here is what I carry specifically for busy city shoots:

  • Wide-Angle Lens: To isolate subjects from background chaos.
  • Compact Gimbal: To move smoothly through dense packs of people.
  • Mini Tripod: For low-angle shots that avoid eye-level crowds.
  • Polarizing Filter: Reduces glare and helps separate subjects from busy backgrounds.

FAQ

What is the best time to film in Paris?

Eiffel Tower and Louvre are emptiest at sunrise (7 AM in summer). Avoid sunset; it is the most crowded time of day.

Can I remove tourists in editing?

Yes, software like After Effects has a "Content-Aware Fill" for video that can remove static people. However, it's time-consuming. It's better to avoid them during filming.

Is it legal to film people in public?

Generally yes (freedom of panorama), but laws vary. In France/Germany, people have strong privacy rights. Avoid close-ups of identifiable faces without permission. Focus on architecture.
