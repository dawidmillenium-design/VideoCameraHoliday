Great sunset footage is made before the sun touches the horizon: arrive 45-60 minutes early, expose for the sky rather than the foreground, and keep filming for 20-30 minutes after sunset into blue hour. Golden hour light is soft and forgiving, but the window is short and the exposure changes by the minute — so you plan the shot list in advance and let the sun do the grading. I've chased sunsets from Santorini to the Chilean coast, and the routine below has never wasted an evening.

My worst sunset was also my best lesson. In Lisbon I strolled up to the Miradouro da Senhora do Monte five minutes before sunset, fumbled with settings while the light peaked, and got home with eleven seconds of usable footage. The next evening I arrived an hour early with a plan and shot an entire sequence that's still in my showreel. Same sun, same city — different process.

⚡ Key Takeaways

  • Arrive 45-60 minutes before sunset; the best light often starts well before the sun gets low.
  • Expose for the sky and let foregrounds go dark — that's what makes sunsets look like sunsets.
  • Lock white balance (daylight or ~5600K) so colors don't shift mid-shot.
  • Silhouettes need a clean horizon line and a subject with a readable outline.
  • Stay for blue hour — the 20-30 minutes after sunset is the most cinematic light of the day.

Plan the Shot Before the Light Exists

Golden hour doesn't wait, so your scouting has to happen in boring daylight. My pre-sunset checklist, refined over years of missed shots:

  • Know the exact sunset time and direction. Any sun-position app shows where the sun will drop, not just when. A beach facing the "wrong" way gives you a glow, not a sun-ball — plan accordingly.
  • Scout the foreground in advance. A sunset with nothing in front of it is a screensaver. Find your palm tree, pier, rooftop or person while you can still see to walk around.
  • Decide your three shots. Mine are usually: wide establishing, a moving shot (slow pan or walk-through), and a person in the light. More than five planned shots and you'll miss half of them fumbling.
  • Charge and clear cards at lunch, not at 6 p.m. Golden hour at the end of a travel day is exactly when batteries and cards run dry.
Field tip: Shoot a rehearsal clip 30 minutes before sunset. It confirms framing, exposes the menu settings you'll need, and — honestly — often looks great itself. Some of my best "golden hour" clips were shot at what I call golden afternoon.

Exposing for Golden Light

The single biggest sunset mistake is letting auto exposure brighten everything. The camera sees a dark foreground and lifts it, which washes the sky to pale nothing. Instead:

  • Expose for the sky. Tap or meter on the bright part of the sky near the sun, then nudge exposure down a third to a full stop. Foregrounds go dark and moody; the sky keeps its color.
  • Lock white balance. Auto white balance will "correct" your gorgeous orange light into gray. Set daylight (~5600K) or cloudy (~6500K) for extra warmth, and leave it there.
  • Keep shutter at double frame rate, ISO at base. As light falls, open aperture or raise ISO — don't drag the shutter below 1/50 or motion turns to smear.
  • Skip the ND at golden hour. The light is finally cooperating; the ND filters from the harsh sunlight guide can stay in the bag until tomorrow's noon.

Watch the light change in stops, not minutes

In the last 15 minutes before sunset, exposure can drop a stop every few minutes. On manual cameras I ride the aperture or ISO between takes; on phones and action cams I re-tap the sky to re-meter every shot. If you're filming a long continuous take — a walking shot along the beach — start slightly underexposed so the brightening sky at the end of the take doesn't clip.

Silhouettes: The Easiest Cinematic Shot in Travel

A silhouette is what happens when you stop fighting the dark foreground and use it. Expose fully for the bright sky, place your subject between you and the brightest part of it, and they become a clean black shape. It looks expensive and costs nothing.

Three rules from a lot of mediocre silhouettes:

  • Read the outline. Arms slightly away from the body, profile faces, hats, flowing clothes — anything that makes the shape legible. Two people hugging read as one blob; holding hands apart reads as a story.
  • Get low. Shooting from a low angle puts your subject against sky instead of against the darker sea or rooftops, which doubles the contrast.
  • Move slowly. A walking silhouette at 24 or 30fps is lovely; the same walk at 60fps played half-speed is the single most-used clip in my travel edits.

Don't Leave: Blue Hour Is the Secret Second Sunset

When the sun drops, most tourists pack up. Stay put. For the next 20-30 minutes the sky turns a deep, even blue, city lights flicker on, and the light goes soft enough that faces and neon signs coexist in one exposure. It's the most cinematic light of the day and half the internet's "sunset" footage is actually shot in it.

Warning: Blue hour punishes auto settings. Auto ISO will climb into noisy territory and auto shutter will drop below 1/50, smearing any motion. Lock shutter at 1/50 (or 1/60), set ISO manually, and accept a darker frame — you can lift shadows a little in the edit, but you can't un-smear motion or un-noise grain. Small-sensor cameras hit this wall first; phones and action cams are often done 10 minutes after sunset, while a mirrorless body keeps going.

This is when I shoot cities: wet streets doubling every light, food stalls glowing, skylines with actual color in the sky instead of black. If you're filming dinner at a night market after sunset, the low-light techniques in our street food filming guide pick up exactly where blue hour ends.

My Golden Hour Shot List, Start to Finish

One evening, one location, in order:

  • T-minus 60 min: Wide establishing shot while light is still strong; b-roll of the location waking up for evening.
  • T-minus 30: People shots — travel companions, vendors, anyone in that soft warm light. This is portrait-perfect light; use it on faces.
  • T-minus 10: Moving shots: slow pans, walk-throughs, gimbal moves toward the light.
  • Sunset: The sun-ball itself, then silhouettes against the brightest sky.
  • T-plus 10-30: Blue hour city and street shots, lights coming on.
  • On the way home: Record a 10-second voice memo of what worked — your future self editing at midnight will thank you. Or keep your hands free and follow the solo tricks in our guide to filming yourself without a crew.

It's the same light-first planning the locals preach in our City Through the Lens series — every city has its one hour a day when it shows off. Your only job is to be there, early, with a charged battery.

Frequently Asked Questions

What settings should I use for sunset video?

Keep shutter at double your frame rate (1/60 for 30fps), ISO at its base value, and white balance locked at daylight (~5600K) or cloudy for extra warmth. Expose for the sky — tap or meter on the bright area near the sun and let the foreground go dark.

Why does my sunset footage look washed out?

Auto exposure is brightening the dark foreground and bleaching the sky, and auto white balance is neutralizing the orange. Fix both: meter on the sky, lower exposure slightly, and lock white balance instead of leaving it on auto.

How long does golden hour actually last?

Rarely a full hour. Near the equator the best light can be 20-30 minutes; in northern summers it can stretch past an hour. Arrive 45-60 minutes before sunset — the warm light usually starts well before the sun gets visibly low.

Is it worth filming after the sun sets?

Absolutely. The 20-30 minutes of blue hour after sunset give a deep blue sky with city lights switched on — the most cinematic light of the day. Lock your shutter and ISO manually, because auto settings will smear motion and add noise in the dim light.
