Dhaka is one of the most densely populated cities on Earth — a relentless, humid, monsoon-soaked metropolis where rickshaws flood the streets and the Buriganga River reflects a chaotic skyline. Filming here is an endurance test. The city's extreme humidity, frequent floods, and non-stop energy create a unique visual language that is both challenging and rewarding. The iconic landmarks — the Pink Palace (Ahsan Manzil), Lalbagh Fort, and the National Parliament — are surrounded by a sea of humanity that never stops moving. The monsoon transforms the city into a dramatic waterworld, while the heat and dust test the limits of your camera's weather-sealing.

In the upcoming Dhaka episode of City Through the Lens, we are searching for a local videographer to answer 15 practical questions about filming in Bangladesh's bustling capital. While we finalize that interview, this guide gives you the essential field notes — from the three locations that define Dhaka's visual identity, to the monsoon strategies that keep your gear safe, and the rickshaw-mounted shots that locals swear by.

⚡ Key Takeaways — Dhaka

  • Best filming seasons: November–February offers the most comfortable weather (20–25°C, low humidity).
  • Top 3 locations: Ahsan Manzil (Pink Palace), Lalbagh Fort, and the National Parliament.
  • Critical permit rule: Drones are banned in most areas. Tripods are allowed but can be problematic in crowds.
  • Airport tip: Hazrat Shahjalal International Airport (DAC) is about 40–90 minutes from the city depending on traffic.

3 Must-Film Locations in Dhaka

Dhaka's landmarks are visually stunning, but capturing them requires navigating crowds, heat, and the city's famous chaos. These three locations offer the best variety — and they're the ones we'll be asking our local interviewee to break down in detail.

1. Ahsan Manzil (Pink Palace)

Ahsan Manzil, also known as the Pink Palace, is a stunning pink-colored palace on the banks of the Buriganga River. It was the official residential palace of the Nawabs of Dhaka and is now a museum. The building's Indo-Saracenic architecture, with its domes and arches, makes it one of Dhaka's most photographed landmarks.

Filming tip: The best time to film Ahsan Manzil is golden hour (just before sunset) when the pink sandstone glows in the warm light. For a unique perspective, take a boat on the Buriganga River — you'll get a stunning reflection of the palace in the water. Tripods are allowed in the grounds but are prohibited inside the museum. A wide-angle lens (16–35mm) is essential for capturing the full facade. Be prepared for crowds — this is one of Dhaka's most popular tourist sites.

2. Lalbagh Fort

Lalbagh Fort is an incomplete 17th-century Mughal fort complex in the heart of Old Dhaka. It includes the Diwan-i-Aam (Hall of Public Audience), a mosque, and the tomb of Bibi Pari — the daughter of the fort's founder. The fort's red sandstone walls and lush gardens offer a peaceful contrast to the chaos of the surrounding city.

Filming tip: The fort is best photographed in early morning (before 9 AM) when the light is soft and the crowds are thin. Use a fast prime lens (like a 35mm f/1.8) to capture the intricate details of the Mughal architecture. Tripods are allowed in the gardens but not inside the mosque. The view from the fort's walls offers a great overhead perspective of Old Dhaka's chaotic streets.

3. National Parliament (Jatiya Sangsad)

The National Parliament building is a modernist masterpiece designed by the American architect Louis Kahn. Its geometric, concrete forms and massive windows create a striking visual contrast to Dhaka's historic architecture. The building is set on a vast campus with reflective pools and gardens.

Filming tip: The best time to film the Parliament is sunset when the concrete glows in the warm light and the reflective pools mirror the building. A wide-angle lens (16–35mm) is essential for capturing the full scale of the structure. Drones are strictly banned near the Parliament. Tripods are allowed in the gardens and surrounding areas, but you cannot enter the building without prior permission. Be aware of security — the Parliament is a sensitive site, and security personnel may ask you to stop filming if you're too close.

Dhaka's Weather Reality for Videographers

Dhaka has a tropical monsoon climate — meaning extreme heat, high humidity, and torrential rains. The monsoon season (June–September) is the most challenging time to film, but also the most dramatic.

  • Winter (November–February): The most pleasant season. Temperatures range from 20–25°C with low humidity. The skies are clear, and the light is beautiful. This is the best time to film — you'll avoid the worst of the heat and rain.
  • Summer (March–May): Extremely hot and humid, with temperatures often exceeding 35°C and humidity above 80%. The heat is brutal on gear and people alike. Shoot early morning (before 8 AM) or late evening (after 5 PM) to avoid the midday sun. A weather-sealed camera and a dust blower are essential.
  • Monsoon (June–September): The city is drenched in rain. The humidity is near 100%, and the streets flood regularly. This is a high-risk, high-reward season — the dramatic clouds, reflections, and waterlogged streets create incredible visuals, but your gear is at constant risk. A weather-sealed camera (like the OM System OM-1) and a silicon rain cover are non-negotiable. Bring multiple microfiber cloths for drying lenses.
Pro tip for monsoon shooting: Dhaka's monsoon floods are a visual gift. Use slow shutter speeds (1/15 – 1/30) to create motion blur in the rain streaks, and push your ISO to 800-1600 to maintain exposure. A circular polarizer (CPL) can cut through glare on wet surfaces. After the shoot, dry your gear thoroughly with a soft cloth and silica gel packs in your camera bag to prevent mould.

Airport Transfer & Gear Logistics

Dhaka's airport is within the city, but traffic is legendary. Plan your transfer carefully to avoid sitting in gridlock with your gear.

  • Hazrat Shahjalal International Airport (DAC): Located about 20 km north of the city center. The best way to get to the city is by prepaid taxi (available at the airport) or ride-hailing apps like Uber and Pathao. The journey can take 40–90 minutes depending on traffic. Avoid the local buses — they're overcrowded and difficult with camera bags.

Pro tip for traffic: If you're flying in, schedule your transfer to avoid peak hours (8–11 AM and 5–8 PM). The roads are often clogged, so consider using the airport shuttle or booking a hotel that offers pick-up services. Keep your camera gear securely packed — the bumpy roads can jostle bags.

Permits, Etiquette & The Reality of Filming in Dhaka

Dhaka is a bustling, chaotic city where filming regulations are often loosely enforced — until they're not. Here's what you need to know to avoid trouble:

  • Drones: Drones are strictly banned in most of Dhaka due to security concerns and the city's high population density. You need permits from the Bangladesh Civil Aviation Authority and local police — a process that can take weeks and is rarely approved for tourists. Do not fly your drone — you risk confiscation and fines.
  • Tripods and gimbals: Tripods are generally allowed in public spaces like the Pink Palace and Lalbagh Fort grounds, but they're difficult to use in crowded areas like Old Dhaka's streets — they block pedestrian flow. A compact gimbal (like the DJI RS 3 Mini) is a much more practical choice for street filming. Be prepared to move quickly — the crowds don't stop for anyone.
  • Filming people: Bangladesh has privacy laws. Always ask permission before filming individuals, especially in close-up shots. The best approach is to be friendly and respectful — a smile and a "Can I film you?" (in Bengali or English) goes a long way. Many locals are happy to be filmed, but some will refuse, and you must respect that. In busy markets, it's often best to shoot the crowds as a whole rather than singling out specific people.
  • Security and public places: Dhaka has a visible police presence, especially around government buildings and monuments. They are generally tolerant of tourists with cameras, but they will intervene if you're blocking pedestrian flow or behaving disruptively. If in doubt, ask a security guard — they're used to tourists and can often guide you to permitted filming spots.
