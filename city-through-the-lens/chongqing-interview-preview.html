Chongqing Filming Guide: Sony A7S III in Fog

One question, asked in the 50 largest metro areas on Earth: how do you really film your hometown? Local shooters answer with gear, settings, light, permits, and etiquette — no lab charts, no stock footage.

Chongqing is China's "mountain city" — a vertiginous, fog-shrouded metropolis where buildings stack on top of each other, light rail trains run through apartment blocks, and the geography is so complex that GPS often fails. Filming here is unlike any other city on Earth. The famous Hongyadong stilt houses, the Yangtze River cable car, and the perpetual fog create a visual identity that feels like a cyberpunk dream. But the challenges are equally unique — the humidity, the lack of open sky, and the sheer verticality of the city make this a serious test for any videographer's gear and skill.

In the upcoming Chongqing episode of City Through the Lens, we are searching for a local videographer to answer 15 practical questions about filming in China's most cinematic city. While we finalize that interview, this guide gives you the essential field notes — from the three locations that define Chongqing's visual identity, to the fog that can either be your enemy or your best friend, and the humidity that destroys unprotected gear.

⚡ Key Takeaways — Chongqing

  • Best filming seasons: Spring (March–May) for comfortable temperatures; Autumn (September–November) for clearer skies.
  • Top 3 locations: Hongyadong (night), Yangtze River Cableway, and Ciqikou Ancient Town.
  • Critical permit rule: Drones are heavily restricted due to the city's complex airspace. Tripods are difficult to use in crowded areas.
  • Airport tip: Jiangbei International Airport (CKG) connects via Metro Line 10 and Line 3 to the city center.

3 Must-Film Locations in Chongqing

Chongqing is a city of layers — both literally and metaphorically. These three locations capture the city's unique vertical essence and are the ones we'll be asking our local interviewee to break down in detail.

1. Hongyadong (Stilt Houses)

Hongyadong is Chongqing's most iconic landmark — a cluster of traditional-style stilt houses built into the cliffs overlooking the Jialing River. At night, the entire complex lights up in brilliant gold and red, creating a scene that looks like it's straight out of Spirited Away. It is the city's most photographed location, and for good reason.

Filming tip: The classic shot is from the Qiansimen Bridge looking south toward Hongyadong. For a less crowded angle, walk along the riverbank on the opposite side of the Jialing River. Drones are banned here — the airspace is restricted. A fast lens (like a 24mm f/1.4) and a gimbal are essential for handheld night work. The area is packed with tourists — the best time to film is just after sunset when the lights first turn on, before the crowds peak around 8 PM.

2. Yangtze River Cableway

The Yangtze River Cableway is a gondola lift that crosses the Yangtze River, connecting the Yuzhong and Nan'an districts. It's one of the few urban cable cars in the world, and the views of the river, the bridges, and the city's vertical skyline are spectacular.

Filming tip: For the best footage, take the cable car from the Yuzhong side (near the Yangtze River Bridge) just before sunset. You'll get the golden light on the skyline on the way out, and the neon lights of the city on the return trip. A wide-angle lens (16–35mm) is ideal for capturing the full panorama. Shoot through the glass — use a polarizing filter to cut reflections. Be aware that the gondola can be crowded, so a compact gimbal like the DJI RS 3 Mini is easier to handle in tight spaces.

3. Ciqikou Ancient Town

Ciqikou is a historic town on the banks of the Jialing River, with a history dating back over 1,000 years. It's a preserved Ming and Qing dynasty village with narrow alleys, traditional architecture, and street vendors selling local snacks. It offers a stark contrast to the futuristic skyscrapers of the city center.

Filming tip: Visit early morning (before 9 AM) to avoid the day-tripping crowds. The narrow alleys and traditional storefronts are perfect for detail-oriented b-roll — use a fast prime lens (like a 35mm f/1.8) to isolate subjects and create shallow depth of field. The riverside walkway offers great views of the Jialing River and the modern city skyline in the distance, creating a classic "old meets new" composition.

Chongqing's Weather Reality for Videographers

Chongqing is known as the "Fog Capital of China" — with an average of over 100 foggy days per year. The city's geography, nestled in a basin at the confluence of two rivers, creates a microclimate that traps moisture and pollution, resulting in a persistent, soft haze that defines the city's visual aesthetic.

  • Spring (March–May): Mild temperatures (15–25°C) but frequent rain and fog. The fog can be used creatively as a natural softbox, but it also limits visibility for wide shots. Anti-fog wipes for your lens are essential.
  • Summer (June–August): Chongqing is one of China's "Three Furnaces" — temperatures often exceed 35°C, with humidity above 80%. The heat and humidity are brutal on gear. Weather-sealed cameras and silicon rain covers are non-negotiable. The upside: dramatic thunderstorms and lush green vegetation.
  • Autumn (September–November): The best time to film. Temperatures drop to a comfortable 15–25°C, and the fog thins out, offering clearer skies. The autumn foliage around the city adds color to your b-roll.
  • Winter (December–February): Cold and damp (temperatures around 5–10°C). The fog is thickest in winter, reducing visibility to just a few hundred meters on some days. This can be used for moody, atmospheric shots — the city looks like it's floating in a cloud.
Pro tip for shooting in fog: Instead of fighting the fog, embrace it as a compositional tool. Use telephoto compression to isolate foreground subjects against the misty background, creating a sense of depth and mystery. In post-production, use a Dehaze tool (in Lightroom, DaVinci Resolve, or Premiere Pro) to add contrast back to your shots — but be careful not to overdo it, or you'll introduce noise and artifacts.

Airport Transfer & Gear Logistics

Chongqing Jiangbei International Airport is the primary gateway, and it's well-connected to the city center.

  • Chongqing Jiangbei International Airport (CKG): Located about 25 km northeast of the city center. The Metro Line 10 connects the airport to the city's main hubs (including Hongyadong and the city center) in about 40 minutes (cost: around ¥10). Metro Line 3 also serves the airport but is slower and more crowded. Both lines have space for luggage, but if you're traveling with a large camera bag, avoid peak hours (8-9 AM and 5-7 PM).

Pro tip for humidity: Chongqing's humidity can cause condensation inside your camera when moving between air-conditioned buildings and the outside heat. Let your gear acclimate — keep your camera in your bag for 10-15 minutes when transitioning between extreme temperatures to prevent fogging on the sensor or lens elements.

Permits, Etiquette & The Reality of Filming in Chongqing

Chongqing is a bustling, modern city, but filming regulations are enforced, and the crowds add an extra layer of complexity. Here's what you need to know:

  • Drones: Chongqing's airspace is complicated due to the two rivers, the mountains, and the military presence. Drones are banned in most central areas, especially near Hongyadong, the cableway, and the bridges. You need permits from the CAAC and local authorities — a process that can take weeks. In practice, do not fly your drone in downtown Chongqing; you will likely have it confiscated.
  • Tripods and gimbals: Tripods are allowed in parks and open areas (like Ciqikou) but are difficult to use in crowded places like Hongyadong — they block the flow of pedestrians. A compact gimbal is the better choice for most street filming. The DJI RS 3 Mini is a popular choice for Chongqing videographers.
  • Filming people: Chinese privacy laws apply. Avoid filming individuals without consent, especially in sensitive areas. When filming in tourist areas, it's best to shoot the crowds as a whole rather than singling out specific people.
  • Vertical city logistics: Chongqing is known for its "8D" geography — buildings at different elevations often don't share the same ground floor. Be prepared for steep stairs, escalators, and elevators. Pack light — a heavy camera bag will tire you out quickly.
