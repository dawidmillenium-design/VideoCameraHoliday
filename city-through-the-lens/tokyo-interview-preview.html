Tokyo Filming Guide: Osmo Pocket 3 in Rain

Tokyo is the ultimate playground for night videography — but it is also one of the most challenging cities to film without looking like a tourist. The neon-lit alleys of Shinjuku, the organized chaos of Shibuya Crossing, and the serene temples of Asakusa demand a completely different approach to gear, settings, and etiquette than you'd use in Paris or New York.

In the upcoming premiere of City Through the Lens, we are searching for a local Tokyo videographer to answer 15 practical questions about filming in Japan's capital. While we finalize that interview, this guide covers everything you need to know before you pack your bag — from the three locations you absolutely must film, to the weather windows that make or break your shoot, and the permit rules that catch 90% of tourists off guard.

⚡ Key Takeaways — Tokyo

  • Best filming seasons: Spring (March–May) for cherry blossoms; Autumn (October–November) for vibrant foliage.
  • Top 3 locations: Sensō-ji Temple, Shibuya Scramble Crossing, and Shinjuku Gyoen.
  • Critical permit rule: Drones are banned in most central areas. Tripods are restricted in temples and private property.
  • Airport tip: Haneda (HND) is just 30 minutes from the city via monorail; Narita (NRT) takes 60 minutes via N'EX.

3 Must-Film Locations in Tokyo

Tokyo is vast, and you could spend weeks chasing shots. But if you have limited time, these three locations give you the most cinematic bang for your buck — and they're the ones we'll be asking our local interviewee about in detail.

1. Sensō-ji Temple (Asakusa)

Tokyo's oldest temple is spectacular at any hour, but the magic happens during blue hour — just after sunset — when the giant red lantern (the chōchin) glows against the twilight sky. The approach through Nakamise-dori, packed with souvenir stalls, offers incredible depth-of-field opportunities if you can time it when the crowds thin out (early morning or late evening).

Filming tip: Tripods are technically prohibited inside the main temple grounds. A pocket gimbal like the DJI Osmo Pocket 3 is your best friend here — it's discreet, stable, and won't draw the attention of security guards.

2. Shibuya Scramble Crossing

The world's busiest pedestrian intersection is a cinematographer's dream and nightmare. The dream: thousands of people crossing in perfect synchronization from all directions. The nightmare: finding an elevated spot that isn't overcrowded.

Filming tip: The second-floor window of the Starbucks is the classic spot, but it's usually packed. The Magnet by Shibuya 109 rooftop offers a less-crowded elevated angle. Our future interviewee will reveal their secret spot — likely a lesser-known parking garage or office building with public access.

3. Shinjuku Gyoen

This spacious national garden offers a serene contrast to the neon chaos of Shinjuku's streets. It's perfect for establishing shots, slow-motion b-roll of leaves falling in autumn, and cherry blossom close-ups in spring. The French, English, and Japanese garden sections give you variety without changing locations.

Filming tip: Check the opening hours (usually 9:00 AM – 4:30 PM). Drones are banned, but handheld gimbals and small tripods are generally tolerated if you're not blocking pathways.

Tokyo's Weather Reality for Videographers

Tokyo has four distinct seasons, and each presents a unique set of challenges and opportunities for filmmakers. Understanding the weather windows is the difference between coming home with portfolio-grade footage and coming home with grey, flat, unusable clips.

  • Spring (March–May): Mild temperatures (10–22°C) and the famous cherry blossoms. Best time for outdoor shoots, but expect crowds at every major park. The rainy season starts in June, so May is your golden window.
  • Summer (June–August): Hot, humid, and rainy. Temperatures regularly hit 30–35°C, with humidity above 80%. Typhoons can appear suddenly. You will need weather-sealed gear or rain covers. The upside: dramatic stormy skies and vibrant green foliage.
  • Autumn (October–November): Crisp, clear air, comfortable temperatures (12–22°C), and stunning red and gold foliage. This is arguably the best time to film — the light is beautiful and the skies are often brilliant blue.
  • Winter (December–February): Clear, dry, and cold (often below freezing at night). The air is crisp, and the low winter sun creates long, dramatic shadows. Battery life suffers in the cold — you'll need multiple spares kept warm in your pocket.
Pro tip for rainy season: Rainy days in Tokyo aren't a disaster — they're an opportunity. Neon lights reflect beautifully on wet pavement, and the crowds thin out. Pack a silicon rain cover for your camera and a microfiber cloth for your lens, and embrace the moody aesthetic.

Airport Transfer & Gear Logistics

Getting your gear safely from the airport to your hotel is the first test of your Tokyo filming trip. Here's what you need to know about Tokyo's two major airports:

  • Haneda Airport (HND): Closer to the city (about 30 minutes). Take the Keikyu Line or the Tokyo Monorail to Hamamatsucho Station, then transfer to the JR Yamanote Line. Cost: around ¥500–¥600. This is the best option if you're carrying heavy camera bags — less time on public transport means less fatigue.
  • Narita Airport (NRT): Further out (about 60 km east). The Narita Express (N'EX) takes about 60 minutes to reach Tokyo Station and costs ¥3,070. The Skyliner is faster (40 minutes to Nippori Station) but only slightly cheaper. If you're traveling with multiple lenses and a drone, consider booking the Airport Limousine Bus — it drops you directly at major hotels and saves you hauling gear up and down station stairs.

Pro tip for your carry-on: Always keep your camera body, primary lens, and gimbal in your carry-on luggage. Checked baggage is handled roughly, and you don't want to arrive in Tokyo with a misaligned gimbal.

Permits, Etiquette & The Unwritten Rules

Tokyo is a city of rules — some written, many unspoken. Violating these can get you politely but firmly escorted away by security, or worse, in trouble with the police. Here's the reality:

  • Drones: Almost entirely banned in central Tokyo. You need a permit from the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism, and even then, flight is restricted near airports, government buildings, and crowded areas. In practice, you will not be flying a drone in Shibuya or Shinjuku. Some parks and riversides are exceptions, but always check the latest regulations.
  • Tripods and gimbals: Tripods are prohibited inside most temples, shrines, and museums. They are also discouraged on busy pedestrian streets (you'll block the flow). A compact gimbal or a tabletop tripod that fits in your pocket is much safer and less likely to attract attention.
  • Filming people: Japanese privacy laws are strict. Do not shove a camera in someone's face. If you want to film a street performer or a shopkeeper, ask first — a simple "Sumimasen, shashin o totte mo ii desu ka?" (Excuse me, may I take a picture/film?) goes a long way. Many people will say no, and you must respect that.
  • Private property: Many backstreets in Shinjuku and Shibuya are private property. Look for signs that say "private road" or "no photography." The famous "Godzilla Road" is a notable exception — it's public, but show courtesy.
