Shanghai is a city of two faces — the colonial-era Bund facing the futuristic skyscrapers of Pudong. It is one of the most photographed skylines on Earth, but capturing it without falling into cliché is a serious challenge for any videographer. The city's famous haze, strict permit enforcement, and relentless crowds demand a strategic approach to gear, timing, and etiquette.

In the upcoming Shanghai episode of City Through the Lens, we are searching for a local videographer to answer 15 practical questions about filming in China's financial capital. While we finalize that interview, this guide gives you the essential field notes — from the three locations that define the city, to the weather windows that turn grey skies into cinematic gold, and the tripod rules that catch most tourists completely off guard.

⚡ Key Takeaways — Shanghai

  • Best filming seasons: Spring (March–May) and Autumn (October–November) for clear skies and comfortable temperatures.
  • Top 3 locations: The Bund, Pudong Skyline (Oriental Pearl Tower), and Yu Garden.
  • Critical permit rule: Tripods are strictly enforced on the Bund. Drones are banned in most central districts.
  • Airport tip: Pudong (PVG) has the Maglev train (world's fastest) — a must-film experience itself.

3 Must-Film Locations in Shanghai

Shanghai offers endless opportunities, but these three locations give you the iconic shots that define the city's visual identity — and they're the ones we'll be asking our local interviewee to break down in detail.

1. The Bund

The Bund is Shanghai's most famous waterfront — a 1.5 km stretch of colonial-era buildings on one side and the futuristic Pudong skyline on the other. The day-to-night timelapse is the classic shot, capturing the transition from misty dawn to neon-lit evening.

Filming tip: Tripods are strictly prohibited on the Bund's pedestrian promenade. Security will ask you to pack them up. The workaround: use a compact gimbal like the DJI RS 3 Mini or shoot handheld with in-body stabilization (IBIS). Alternatively, set up on the opposite side of the Huangpu River in Pudong for a tripod-friendly perspective.

2. Pudong Skyline (Oriental Pearl Tower & Shanghai Tower)

Pudong is the futuristic heart of Shanghai. The Oriental Pearl Tower is the most recognizable landmark, but the Shanghai Tower (the world's second-tallest building) offers breathtaking observation decks. Filming the skyline from the Bund is classic, but shooting from Pudong looking back at the historic Bund offers a fresh perspective.

Filming tip: The Lujiazui pedestrian bridge offers excellent angles for wide shots of the skyscrapers. For a unique perspective, take a Huangpu River cruise at sunset — the moving platform gives you dynamic foreground motion against the static skyline.

3. Yu Garden

Nestled in the old city, Yu Garden is a classical Ming-dynasty garden with pavilions, rockeries, and lotus ponds. It offers a serene contrast to the high-tech chaos of Pudong. The intricate lattice windows and curved eaves provide perfect framing opportunities for detail-oriented b-roll.

Filming tip: Visit early morning (right as it opens) to avoid the tourist crowds that flood the garden by 10 AM. A fast prime lens (like a 35mm f/1.8) helps isolate subjects against the busy backgrounds and handles the shaded areas well.

Shanghai's Weather Reality for Videographers

Shanghai has a subtropical monsoon climate — meaning hot, humid summers and chilly, damp winters. The haze and fog are perhaps the most defining weather feature for filmmakers, acting as a natural diffusion filter that can either ruin your shot or make it look incredibly moody.

  • Spring (March–May): Mild and comfortable (10–22°C). The city blooms with flowers, and the skies are relatively clear. This is a prime filming window, though you'll share the locations with millions of domestic tourists during the May Day holiday.
  • Summer (June–August): Hot, humid, and rainy. Temperatures often hit 35°C with humidity above 80%. The plum rain season (梅雨, Méiyǔ) in June and July brings persistent drizzle — great for neon reflections but tough on unprotected gear. Typhoons can also hit, offering dramatic storm skies.
  • Autumn (September–November): Arguably the best time to film. Clear blue skies, comfortable 15–25°C temperatures, and lower humidity. The Qixi Festival and Mid-Autumn Festival add cultural color to your b-roll.
  • Winter (December–February): Cold and damp (temperatures around 5–10°C), with frequent grey skies and haze. The low winter sun creates long, dramatic shadows, but battery life suffers in the cold. The haze can be used creatively as a softbox for portrait work.
Pro tip for haze and smog: Instead of fighting the haze, embrace it. Shoot telephoto compression shots across the river — the haze layers the background, creating a beautiful sense of depth. Use a circular polarizer (CPL) to cut through some of the atmospheric reflections, and consider a Dehaze adjustment in post-production (DaVinci Resolve or Lightroom) to recover contrast.

Airport Transfer & Gear Logistics

Shanghai has two major airports, and your choice can significantly impact how quickly you get your gear to your hotel.

  • Pudong International Airport (PVG): The main international gateway, located about 30 km east of the city. The Maglev train is a must-film experience — it reaches 430 km/h (267 mph) and gets you to Longyang Road Station in just 8 minutes (cost: ¥50). From there, transfer to Metro Line 2 or 7. The Maglev itself is a great piece of B-roll for any Shanghai travel video.
  • Hongqiao International Airport (SHA): Closer to the city center, primarily for domestic flights and some regional international routes. It connects directly via Metro Line 2 and Line 10, taking about 30-45 minutes to reach the Bund area. It's less flashy than Pudong but more convenient for quick trips.

Pro tip for security: Chinese airport security is thorough. Keep your gimbal batteries and camera bodies in your carry-on. Lithium batteries are strictly prohibited in checked luggage, and you may be asked to power on your camera to prove it's functional.

Permits, Etiquette & The Reality of Filming in Shanghai

Shanghai is a modern, efficient city, but filming regulations are strict and enforcement is consistent. Here's what you need to know:

  • Drones: Largely banned in the city center. The Bund, Pudong, and most popular parks are no-fly zones. You need to register your drone with the Civil Aviation Administration of China (CAAC) and obtain specific permits for commercial or even recreational flights in permitted zones (usually outside the Outer Ring Road). In practice, do not expect to fly a drone in the downtown area.
  • Tripods and gimbals: As mentioned, tripods are banned on the Bund's walking promenade. The rule is enforced by security guards who patrol regularly. A small tabletop tripod or a sturdy monopod is sometimes tolerated if used discreetly, but a full-size tripod will get you flagged. Gimbals (like the DJI RS series) are generally permitted if used respectfully.
  • Filming people: China has strict privacy laws. Avoid filming individuals without their consent, especially in sensitive areas like government buildings or military installations. When shooting street scenes, it's best to focus on the architecture and crowds in a general sense rather than singling out specific people. If you want to interview someone, always ask permission first.
  • Subway and public transport: Filming in the metro is allowed, but using a flash or a large rig is discouraged. Be mindful of commuters — do not block doors or escalators.
