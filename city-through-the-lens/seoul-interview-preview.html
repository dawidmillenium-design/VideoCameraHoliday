Seoul Filming Guide: Sony FX6 in Neon

Seoul is a city of extreme contrasts — ancient palaces stand beside hyper-modern skyscrapers, K-pop concerts erupt in stadiums while traditional tea houses sit quietly in Bukchon Hanok Village. Filming here requires versatility: one moment you're shooting a serene palace at sunset, the next you're capturing the chaotic neon energy of Hongdae or Gangnam. The city's dynamic visual language—from the historic beauty of Gyeongbokgung to the digital billboards of Myeongdong—offers endless creative possibilities, but also presents unique challenges for videographers, especially when it comes to permits and crowd control.

In the upcoming Seoul episode of City Through the Lens, we are searching for a local videographer to answer 15 practical questions about filming in South Korea's capital. While we finalize that interview, this guide gives you the essential field notes — from the three locations that capture Seoul's dual identity, to the cold winters that drain batteries, and the concert etiquette that every fan-cam shooter needs to know.

⚡ Key Takeaways — Seoul

  • Best filming seasons: Spring (April–May) for cherry blossoms; Autumn (September–November) for crisp, clear skies and fall foliage.
  • Top 3 locations: Gyeongbokgung Palace, Bukchon Hanok Village, and Namsan Tower (with Hongdae for nightlife).
  • Critical permit rule: Drones are heavily restricted in central Seoul. Tripods are allowed in most public spaces but can be problematic in crowded areas.
  • Airport tip: Incheon International Airport (ICN) is about 45–60 minutes from the city by AREX train or bus.

3 Must-Film Locations in Seoul

Seoul is a city that rewards exploration. These three locations give you a taste of its historical depth, urban energy, and panoramic beauty — and they're the ones we'll be asking our local interviewee to break down in detail.

1. Gyeongbokgung Palace

Gyeongbokgung is Seoul's largest and most iconic palace, dating back to 1395. The main gate (Gwanghwamun), the throne hall (Geunjeongjeon), and the surrounding gardens are some of the most photographed sites in the city. The palace is especially stunning during cherry blossom season and when the fall foliage turns the grounds into a sea of red and gold.

Filming tip: The best time to film the palace is just before sunset when the golden light warms the stone and wood. For a unique angle, go to the National Palace Museum across the street for elevated shots. Tripods are allowed in the palace grounds, but you'll need to pay a small fee for a filming permit if you're using a tripod for extended periods. The changing of the guard ceremony (held at Gwanghwamun Gate) is a great B-roll opportunity — get there early for a front-row position.

2. Bukchon Hanok Village

Bukchon is a residential village of traditional Korean houses (hanok) nestled between Gyeongbokgung and Changdeokgung palaces. The narrow, winding streets and tiled roofs offer a glimpse into Seoul's past. It's a favorite location for filmmakers and photographers, but it's also a real neighborhood where people live — so respect is paramount.

Filming tip: Visit early morning (before 9 AM) to avoid the crowds. The best views are from the higher streets looking down over the village towards the city skyline. A wide-angle lens (16–35mm) is ideal for capturing the sweeping rooflines. Do not enter private property or block doorways. Be courteous — a simple "안녕하세요" (hello) and a smile go a long way.

3. Namsan Seoul Tower

Namsan Tower (also called Namsan Seoul Tower) is a communications tower on Mount Namsan, offering panoramic views of the city. The tower itself is a landmark, and the observation decks provide spectacular 360-degree views of Seoul's sprawling urban landscape, especially at sunset and night when the city lights up.

Filming tip: The best time to go is about 45 minutes before sunset so you can capture the transition from daylight to blue hour to night. A telephoto lens (70–200mm) can isolate specific landmarks like the Han River or Lotte World Tower. Tripods are allowed on the outdoor observation deck but can be crowded — a sturdy monopod is a good compromise. If you want a less touristy angle, hike up the mountain trail (about 30 minutes) for a ground-level view of the tower with the city below.

Seoul's Weather Reality for Videographers

Seoul has a humid continental climate with four distinct seasons — and each season brings its own set of challenges and opportunities for filmmakers.

  • Spring (April–May): Mild temperatures (10–22°C) and the famous cherry blossoms. The city is awash in pink and white, but the crowds are intense. Early morning shoots are essential to avoid the masses. The pollen count is high, which can affect air quality and visibility — keep lens wipes handy.
  • Summer (June–August): Hot and humid (often above 30°C) with occasional monsoon rains (장마, jangma) in July and August. The humidity can fog lenses, and the rain can create dramatic reflections on pavement — but you'll need rain covers and anti-fog wipes. Typhoons can also bring strong winds and rain, making outdoor shoots risky.
  • Autumn (September–November): Arguably the best time to film. Clear, crisp skies, comfortable temperatures (15–25°C), and stunning fall foliage — especially in the mountains around the city. The light is beautiful for most of the day, and the air is often pollution-free after the summer rains.
  • Winter (December–February): Cold and dry (temperatures often drop below freezing, with occasional snow). The skies are often brilliant blue, and the low winter sun creates long, dramatic shadows. Battery life plummets — keep spare batteries in your inner pocket. Snow-covered palaces and temples offer a magical winter wonderland aesthetic.
Pro tip for Seoul's air quality: Seoul occasionally experiences fine dust (미세먼지, misemeonji) days when the air quality is poor. On these days, use a UV or clear filter to protect your lens from particles, and consider using a Dehaze tool in post-production to restore contrast. Alternatively, embrace the haze for moody, atmospheric shots — the city looks like a watercolor painting.

Airport Transfer & Gear Logistics

Seoul is served by two main airports — Incheon International (ICN) for most international flights, and Gimpo (GMP) for domestic and some regional international routes.

  • Incheon International Airport (ICN): Located about 50 km west of Seoul. The AREX (Airport Railroad Express) is the fastest option — the express train takes about 43 minutes to Seoul Station (cost: around ¥9,000 KRW, ~$7 USD). The all-stop train is cheaper but takes about 1 hour. Both trains have dedicated luggage space, but if you're carrying a large camera bag, the express train is more comfortable. Airport limousine buses also run to major hotels and districts (like Gangnam and Hongdae) — they're a good option if your hotel is near a bus stop.
  • Gimpo Airport (GMP): Closer to the city, primarily for domestic flights (e.g., from Jeju Island). The airport is connected to the Seoul subway system via Line 5 and Line 9, making it easy to reach the city center in about 30 minutes.

Pro tip for security: Incheon Airport has strict security. Keep your camera bodies, lenses, and gimbal batteries in your carry-on. Lithium batteries are not allowed in checked luggage. You may be asked to power on your camera, so keep your batteries charged.

Permits, Etiquette & The Reality of Filming in Seoul

Seoul is a modern, technology-forward city, but filming regulations are enforced, and cultural sensitivities matter. Here's what you need to know:

  • Drones: Drones are heavily restricted in Seoul. The city is within the controlled airspace of Gimpo and Incheon airports, and most of the city is a no-fly zone. You need to register your drone with the Korea Civil Aviation Authority and obtain permits for specific flight zones — a process that can take weeks. In practice, do not fly your drone in central Seoul — you risk confiscation and fines.
  • Tripods and gimbals: Tripods are generally allowed in public parks and open spaces (like Namsan Park) but can be problematic in crowded areas like Myeongdong or Hongdae — they block pedestrian flow. A compact gimbal or a monopod is the better choice for street filming. The DJI RS 3 Mini is a popular choice for Seoul videographers.
  • Filming people: South Korea has strict privacy laws (the Personal Information Protection Act). Avoid filming individuals without their explicit consent, especially in close-up shots. When filming in public places, it's best to shoot crowds as a whole rather than singling out specific people. If you want to interview someone or feature them prominently, always ask permission first.
  • Concert and performance etiquette: If you're filming a K-pop concert, fan-cam etiquette is strict. Cameras with detachable lenses are often prohibited in concert venues — check the venue's policy. If allowed, use silent shutter modes and avoid using flash, which can distract performers and other audience members. A long-zoom compact (like the Sony RX100 VII) is often the best choice for discreet concert filming.
