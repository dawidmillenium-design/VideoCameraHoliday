Moscow is a city of dramatic contrasts — onion domes and Stalinist skyscrapers, Soviet-era brutalist blocks and gleaming modern business towers. It is the largest city on the European continent, and filming here means navigating a complex mix of historic grandeur, strict regulations, and a climate that ranges from sweltering summers to bitterly cold winters[reference:0].

In the upcoming Moscow episode of City Through the Lens, we are searching for a local videographer to answer 15 practical questions about filming in Russia's capital. While we finalize that interview, this guide gives you the essential field notes — from the three locations that define the city's visual identity, to the local weather patterns that can test your gear, and the permit rules that catch most visitors off guard.

⚡ Key Takeaways — Moscow

  • Best filming seasons: Late May–June for White Nights and soft evening light[reference:1]; September–October for golden autumn colours and cinematic fog[reference:2].
  • Top 3 locations: Red Square & St. Basil's Cathedral, Gorky Park, and the Moscow River embankments.
  • Critical permit rule: Professional filming on Red Square requires advance permission — handheld personal use is usually tolerated[reference:3]. Drones over 250g need special authorization from aviation authorities[reference:4].
  • Airport tip: Sheremetyevo (SVO), Domodedovo (DME), or Vnukovo (VKO) connect to the city centre via Aeroexpress trains in 40–50 minutes.

3 Must-Film Locations in Moscow

Local videographers often use gimbals for smooth walking shots in Gorky Park.

Moscow's landmarks are among the most recognisable in the world. But capturing them without falling into cliché requires local knowledge. Here are the three essential locations — and the insider tips that make the difference.

1. Red Square & St. Basil's Cathedral

Red Square is the heart of Moscow — a vast cobblestone plaza flanked by the Kremlin walls, the State Historical Museum, and the iconic St. Basil's Cathedral with its colourful onion domes. It is Moscow's most filmed location, but getting a clean shot without crowds is a challenge.

Filming tip: The best time to shoot Red Square is at dawn — between 4 and 5 AM in summer, the square is nearly empty, and the soft morning light illuminates St. Basil's domes beautifully[reference:5]. For evening shoots, the blue hour (about 20:30–21:24) offers stunning twilight colours[reference:6]. Drones are strictly banned over Red Square and the Kremlin. Tripods may be flagged as "professional equipment" — a compact gimbal or handheld shooting is your safest bet[reference:7]. A wide-angle lens (like a 16–35mm) is essential for capturing the full scale of the square[reference:8].

2. Gorky Park

Gorky Park is Moscow's most famous public park — a sprawling green space along the Moscow River that was completely renovated in the 2010s. It offers a mix of Soviet-era monuments, modern art installations, and endless opportunities for people-watching and lifestyle B-roll.

Filming tip: The park is busiest on weekends — visit on a weekday morning for quieter shots. The Crimean Bridge and the Muzeon Art Park (open-air sculpture garden) are excellent backdrops. For dynamic footage, film the rollerbladers and cyclists along the river embankment — a gimbal is essential for smooth tracking shots. In autumn, the park's golden foliage creates a cinematic palette that's hard to beat[reference:9].

3. Moscow River Embankments & the Kremlin from the Water

The Moscow River cuts through the city, offering unique perspectives of the Kremlin, St. Basil's, and the modern Moscow City business district. The Bolshoy Moskvoretsky Bridge, just behind Red Square, is a favourite spot for photographers[reference:10].

Filming tip: For a truly unique angle, take a river boat tour — the views of the Kremlin walls and St. Basil's from the water are unforgettable. A telephoto lens (70–200mm) lets you compress the skyline and isolate details[reference:11]. Golden hour (just after sunrise or before sunset) is the best time for warm, cinematic light[reference:12]. During the White Nights in late June, the sky stays in a surreal golden twilight until 2 AM — a dream for night videographers[reference:13].

Moscow's Weather Reality for Videographers

Moscow has a humid continental climate — long, cold winters and short, warm summers. Understanding the seasons is essential for protecting your gear and timing your shoots.

  • Winter (December–February): Brutally cold, with average temperatures around -12°C and occasional dips to -25°C[reference:14]. Snow transforms the city into a winter wonderland, but battery life plummets in the cold. Keep spare batteries in your inner pocket to keep them warm. Use a weather-sealed camera and carry lens wipes for when you move between indoors and outdoors (condensation is a real risk).
  • Spring (April–May): Temperatures rise from 5°C to 20°C. The city thaws, and the parks begin to bloom. Late May is a sweet spot — soft, even natural light and pleasant temperatures[reference:15].
  • Summer (June–August): Warm to hot (15–30°C), with long daylight hours. Late June brings the White Nights — the sky stays lit until late, offering extended golden and blue hours[reference:16]. The downside: summer crowds, especially at Red Square.
  • Autumn (September–October): Arguably the best time to film. Crisp temperatures (5–15°C), clear skies, and the city's famous golden foliage. Cinematic fog often rolls in during October mornings[reference:17], adding mood and depth to your shots.
Pro tip for cold-weather filming: If you're shooting in winter, invest in a camera rain cover — it doubles as a snow cover. Keep your camera in a dry bag when moving between indoors and outdoors to prevent condensation. In post-production, use a Dehaze tool to cut through winter smog and restore contrast.

Airport Transfer & Gear Logistics

Moscow has three major international airports. Here's what you need to know:

  • Sheremetyevo (SVO): The busiest airport, located about 30 km northwest of the city. The Aeroexpress train connects to Belorussky Railway Station in about 40 minutes — it's efficient, clean, and camera-bag friendly. Taxis and ride-hailing apps (Yandex Go) are also available but can get stuck in traffic.
  • Domodedovo (DME): Located about 45 km south of the city. The Aeroexpress takes about 50 minutes to Paveletsky Railway Station.
  • Vnukovo (VKO): The smallest of the three, about 30 km southwest. The Aeroexpress takes about 40 minutes to Kievsky Railway Station.

Pro tip for security: Russian airport security is thorough. Keep your gimbal batteries, camera bodies, and lenses in your carry-on. Lithium batteries are strictly prohibited in checked luggage. You may be asked to power on your camera to prove it's functional — so keep your batteries charged.

Permits, Etiquette & The Reality of Filming in Moscow

Moscow has strict filming regulations that catch many visitors off guard. Here's what you need to know:

  • Red Square & the Kremlin: Filming on Red Square is allowed for personal, non-commercial use without a permit[reference:18]. However, using professional equipment (tripods, large rigs, external microphones) may be flagged as commercial activity — you risk being asked to stop or facing a fine[reference:19]. Handheld shooting with a compact camera or smartphone is generally tolerated. Tripods are prohibited without prior permission[reference:20].
  • Drones: Drone regulations in Russia are strict. Drones weighing over 250g require special authorization from the Russian aviation authorities[reference:21]. In practice, drones are banned over central Moscow, including Red Square, the Kremlin, and government buildings. Flying a drone without permission can result in confiscation and significant fines[reference:22].
  • Filming government buildings & military sites: It is illegal to film government buildings, military installations, and sensitive infrastructure. Avoid pointing your camera at the Kremlin walls, government ministry buildings, or any site with security personnel.
  • Filming people: Always ask permission before filming individuals, especially in religious sites (churches, cathedrals) and residential areas. Russian privacy laws are strict, and street photography without consent can lead to confrontations.
  • Recent restrictions: Moscow authorities have introduced bans on publishing photos and videos of the consequences of terrorist attacks and drone strikes[reference:23]. While this primarily affects news reporting, it's a reminder that sensitive content should not be shared online without careful consideration.
