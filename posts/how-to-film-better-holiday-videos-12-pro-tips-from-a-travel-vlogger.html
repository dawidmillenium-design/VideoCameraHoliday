How to Film Better Holiday Videos: 12 Pro Tips from a Travel Vlogger

Composition, lighting, storytelling & editing tricks that make amateur footage look cinematic

After filming across 15 countries and making every amateur mistake imaginable, I have distilled the 12 techniques that transform shaky phone clips into cinematic travel memories. Before applying these tips, make sure you have the right camera for your holiday style.

Composition Tips

1. Rule of Thirds (Not Center)

Amateur: Subject in the center of every shot.

Pro: Place the subject at the intersection of imaginary grid lines. Most cameras have a grid overlay — turn it on.

Example: Filming a temple. Place the temple at the left third intersection, with the sky occupying the top two-thirds. More dynamic than centered.

2. Leading Lines

Use natural lines in the environment to draw the viewer’s eye: - Roads leading to a mountain - Railings guiding to a viewpoint - Shorelines pointing to a sunset

Example: Stand at the start of a pier. Film along the railing toward the ocean. The railing “leads” the viewer into the scene.

3. Layer Your Shots

Amateur: One plane of depth (subject against a wall).

Pro: Three layers — foreground, midground, background.

Example: Filming a street market. - Foreground: A hanging lantern (slightly out of focus) - Midground: A vendor preparing food - Background: Crowded street with signs

This creates depth that flat footage lacks.

Lighting Tips

4. Golden Hour is Everything

The best light: Sunrise and sunset (first/last hour of daylight).

Why: Warm, soft, directional light. Long shadows add depth. Everything looks cinematic.

The worst light: Midday sun (11am-3pm).

Why: Harsh shadows, blown highlights, squinting subjects.

Solution: Film in shade during midday. Or use the Sony ZV-1 II’s built-in ND filter to shoot wide open in bright sun.

5. Backlight for Drama

Place the sun BEHIND your subject for: - Silhouettes at sunset - Golden rim light around hair - Lens flare for cinematic effect

Example: Film a person walking toward a temple at sunrise. The sun behind them creates a glowing outline. Expose for the sky, letting the person become a silhouette.

Movement & Stabilization

6. The “Ninja Walk”

Walking footage is inherently shaky. The ninja walk minimizes bounce:

  1. Bend your knees slightly
  2. Step heel-to-toe (not flat-footed)
  3. Keep the camera close to your body
  4. Move slowly — speed amplifies shake

Better solution: Use a camera with built-in stabilization. The DJI Osmo Pocket 3 makes walking footage look gimbal-smooth automatically.

7. Mix Static and Moving Shots

Amateur: All handheld, all moving.

Pro: 70% static, 30% moving.

Static shots (tripod, resting on a surface) let the viewer absorb the scene. Moving shots add energy. The contrast between the two makes both more effective.

Example: - Static: Temple facade (5 seconds) - Moving: Walking through the gate (3 seconds) - Static: Interior detail (5 seconds) - Moving: Following a monk walking (3 seconds)

Storytelling Techniques

8. Film the “B-Roll”

B-roll is the supporting footage that tells the story: - Hands holding a map - Feet walking on cobblestones - Local food being prepared - Signs and street names - Reaction shots (laughing, pointing, amazed)

The rule: For every “hero shot” (you in front of a landmark), film 5 B-roll clips.

Example: Visiting a market. - Hero: You walking through the market - B-roll: Close-up of spices, vendor’s hands, your face reacting to smells, price signs, bags being filled

9. The 5-Shot Sequence

Professional documentary filmmakers use this formula:

  1. Wide shot: Establish the location
  2. Medium shot: Show the subject in context
  3. Close-up: Detail or emotion
  4. Cutaway: Related detail (hands, objects)
  5. Reaction: Someone’s response

Example: Cooking class in Thailand. 1. Wide: Entire kitchen classroom 2. Medium: You at the stove 3. Close-up: Hands chopping ingredients 4. Cutaway: Steam rising from wok 5. Reaction: Your face tasting the result

This 30-second sequence tells a complete story.

10. Start and End Strong

The first 3 seconds determine if viewers keep watching.

Strong opens: - A surprising moment (monkey jumping on camera) - A beautiful shot (sunrise over mountains) - A question (“What does $2 get you in Bangkok?”)

Strong endings: - A callback to the opening - A summary shot - A “what is next” tease

Audio Tips

11. Audio Matters More Than Video

Viewers forgive shaky footage. They do not forgive bad audio.

Common audio problems: - Wind noise (ruins outdoor footage) - Echo (indoor spaces) - Traffic noise (cities) - Camera handling noise

Solutions: - Use a dead cat windscreen outdoors - Film close to your subject (audio drops with distance) - Use an external mic if vlogging (Rode VideoMicro II is excellent) - Record “room tone” (30 seconds of ambient sound) for editing

Editing Tricks

12. Cut on Motion

The most professional-looking edits happen DURING movement, not between static shots.

Example: - Shot 1: Person walking left (cut as they exit frame) - Shot 2: Person continuing to walk left (entering frame)

The motion carries the viewer through the cut. It feels seamless.

Bonus tip: Match the direction of movement between shots. If someone walks left in shot 1, they should continue left in shot 2. Reversing direction is jarring.

Quick Reference: Shot Checklist

Before filming any location, capture:

  • [ ] Wide establishing shot (show the location)
  • [ ] Medium shot (subject in context)
  • [ ] Close-up detail (texture, emotion)
  • [ ] B-roll (5 supporting clips)
  • [ ] Reaction shot (someone’s response)
  • [ ] Transition shot (movement to next location)

FAQ

How do I make holiday videos look cinematic? Film at golden hour, use rule of thirds, add depth, mix static/moving shots, capture B-roll, and use stabilization.

What is B-roll and why do I need it? B-roll is supporting footage (details, reactions, environment). For every hero shot, film 5 B-roll clips.

How do I reduce shake in walking footage? Use “ninja walk” (bend knees, heel-to-toe) or a camera with built-in stabilization like DJI Pocket 3.

Best time to film outdoors? Golden hour (first/last hour of daylight). Avoid 11am-3pm harsh sun.
