Understanding Depth Requirements

Your choice of camera depends entirely on how deep you plan to go. Here's what each activity typically involves:

🌊 Depth Requirements by Activity

Snorkeling Surface to 3m (10ft) — Most coral viewing happens here
Freediving 3m to 20m (10-65ft) — Holding breath, no tank
Scuba Diving 10m to 40m (30-130ft) — Recreational limits
Technical Diving 40m+ (130ft+) — Requires specialized equipment

Most holiday underwater activities fall into snorkeling and recreational scuba (0-30m). This is the sweet spot where modern waterproof cameras and affordable housings excel.

Color Loss at Depth

Water absorbs light differently than air. As you descend, colors disappear in a predictable order:

Red Gone by 5m
Orange Gone by 10m
Yellow Gone by 20m
Green Gone by 30m

By 10m, everything looks blue-green. By 20m, it's mostly blue. This is why raw underwater footage often looks disappointing — the camera is capturing accurately, but the light simply isn't there.

Essential Filters & Lighting

🔴 Red Filters (Essential for 3-15m)

Red filters compensate for lost red light at shallow depths. They screw onto your housing or clip over the lens. Essential for:

  • Tropical snorkeling (3-8m)
  • Shallow reef dives
  • Pool training videos

Top pick: PolarPro Red Filter for GoPro/DJI (~$30) — optically coated glass, not cheap plastic.

🟠 Magenta Filters (For Green Water)

In green water (lakes, quarries, northern oceans), red filters make things worse. Use a magenta filter instead to restore natural tones.

Top pick: SRP Magenta Filter (~$35) — available for most action camera housings.

💡 Video Lights (Essential for 15m+)

Below 15m, filters can't restore colors that don't exist. You need artificial light. Even a small video light (500-1000 lumens) makes a massive difference.

  • Budget: SeaLife Sea Dragon Mini 650 (~$80) — 650 lumens, compact
  • Mid-range: OrcaTorch D530 (~$120) — 1300 lumens, wide beam
  • Pro: Bigblue VL4200P (~$350) — 4200 lumens, professional quality

🎨 Color Correction in Post

Even with filters and lights, you'll need some color correction. The easiest approach:

  • GoPro/DJI: Use the auto white balance "underwater" mode if available
  • Editing: Raise reds and oranges, reduce blues slightly
  • Software: DaVinci Resolve (free) has excellent color tools; Premiere Pro's Lumetri Color works well too

Must-Have Underwater Accessories

  • Floating hand grip — Essential. If you drop your camera, it floats instead of sinking to the abyss. ~$15
  • Anti-fog inserts — Prevent lens fogging in humid conditions. Replace every few dives. ~$8 for 12 pack
  • Red filter — As discussed above. Match to your housing. ~$30
  • Spare batteries — Cold water drains batteries faster. Carry 2-3 extras. ~$25 each
  • Microfiber cloth — For drying lens and housing between dives. ~$5
  • Silica gel packets — Store in your camera case to absorb moisture. ~$10 for 50 pack
  • Lanyard — Attach camera to your BCD or wrist. Never hold a camera with a loose grip in current. ~$10

Underwater Shooting Techniques

🐠 Get Close, Then Get Closer

Water reduces contrast, color, and sharpness. The closer you are to your subject, the better your footage. For macro subjects (nudibranchs, small fish), get within 30cm if possible.

☀️ Shoot with the Sun Behind You

Underwater light is precious. Position yourself so the sun illuminates your subject. Shooting into the light creates silhouettes — artistic, but rarely what you want for documentary footage.

🤫 Move Slowly and Steady

Fast movements stir up sand, scare fish, and create shaky footage. Move like you're in slow motion. Let curious fish approach you rather than chasing them.

🎬 Shoot in Short Clips

Underwater footage gets boring fast if it's all wide shots of blue water. Mix in:

  • Wide establishing shots (coral reef panorama)
  • Medium shots (fish swimming near coral)
  • Close-ups (fish eye, coral texture, your dive buddy)
  • Surface shots (entering/exiting water, boat silhouette)

⏱️ Time Your Dives

The best underwater light is 10am-2pm when the sun is highest. Avoid early morning and late afternoon dives for video — the light is too dim and too blue.

💡 Pro Tip: If you're snorkeling, shoot on the breath hold descent, not at the surface. Even 1-2 meters down, the water is clearer, colors are better, and fish are less skittish. Practice holding your breath and equalizing before your trip.

Post-Dive Camera Care

Salt water is corrosive. Proper care extends your camera's life:

  1. Rinse immediately — Fresh water rinse within 30 minutes of exiting. Pay attention to buttons, dials, and seams.
  2. Soak if possible — A 10-minute soak in fresh water dissolves salt crystals in crevices.
  3. Dry thoroughly — Air dry completely before opening battery/card doors. Use a microfiber cloth, not paper towels (they leave fibers).
  4. Remove battery and card — Let the compartments air out. Moisture trapped inside causes corrosion.
  5. Check O-rings — For housings, inspect O-rings for sand, hair, or damage. Lightly grease with silicone grease.
  6. Store with silica gel — Keep moisture-absorbing packets in your camera case.
🚨 Never Do This: Don't charge a wet camera. Don't open a housing on a wet boat deck. Don't leave your camera in direct sun after a dive — rapid heating can cause condensation inside the lens. Don't use a hair dryer — the heat damages seals.
